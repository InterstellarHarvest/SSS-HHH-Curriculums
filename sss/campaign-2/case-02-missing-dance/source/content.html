Mission question

Everything in the garden reads normal and the pollen is healthy, so why do the lyreflower buds keep dying before they open?

Case briefing

The Telluvian embassy gave a lyreflower to a sealed botanical garden on Mars. It bloomed beautifully for a month, then stopped. Buds still form, but they dry out and die before they open. The curator has adjusted light, nutrients, humidity and soil, and has tried pollinating the flower by hand three times. Nothing has worked. A Telluvian researcher at the garden appears to know why, but says their culture does not allow them to explain it to outsiders.

Reasoning rule

Something can be present and still not be doing anything. Watch for the difference between a thing that is missing and a thing that never happens. What this flower does is something the investigation recorded, not a rule about flowers on Earth.

Case closed — what it means

Nothing in this garden was broken, missing or unhealthy. The pollen was alive, the openings were already there, and every reading was normal. What was missing was not a thing at all — it was an event. Some living things need something to happen, not just to be present. When a checklist says everything is fine and something still fails, ask what is supposed to happen that is not happening.

Teacher Guide

Lesson overview

Students investigate why a Telluvian lyreflower stopped setting fruit in a sealed Martian garden where every reading is normal, the pollen is viable at 98%, and hand pollination was tried three times without success. The case turns on a distinction students rarely meet: the thing that is missing is not a substance or a structure but an event. It also asks them to work with a source who knows the answer and cannot state it.

Built for grades 6–8 and adapting upward for grades 9–12 and adult learners. It runs with or without gameplay; the evidence ledger on page 4 lets a class complete every task from the printed evidence alone.

Learning target

I can explain why a plant with everything it needs can still fail to reproduce, using evidence from five sources, and I can design a trial that tests a physical stimulus safely rather than guessing one number.

Measurable objectives

  • Distinguish a missing thing from a missing event.
  • Treat a repeated negative result as evidence rather than a dead end.
  • Explain that a specialised flower structure and a specific pollinator behaviour together affect whether reproduction succeeds.
  • State that pollen release depends on frequency, amplitude, duration and coupling together, never on one number alone.
  • Separate established Earth science about buzz pollination from measurements recorded for this alien species.
  • Plan an independent line of evidence when a source cannot supply information directly.
  • Design a monitored trial with a damage limit and a stop rule.

Success criteria

Nothing is called broken

Answers say the pollen is viable, the pores are already present, and every reading is normal. The failure is located at one step, not blamed on plant health.

Pores are never described as opening

The pores exist before anything touches the flower. Nothing unseals, unblocks or opens.

124 Hz never stands alone

Every mention sits beside amplitude, duration and coupling, and stays a measurement for this species rather than a universal figure.

The boundary is respected

The researcher is treated as a professional working within a cultural constraint, not as an obstacle or a keeper of secrets.

Standards alignment and limitation

Direct assessment: MS-LS1-4. Students use evidence and reasoning to explain how a specialised plant structure — the poricidal cone, in Task 3 — and a characteristic animal behaviour — the lyre-moth’s hovering wingbeat, supplied in the Task 5 database row and defined in the vocabulary — together affect the probability of successful reproduction. Tasks 3, 5, 6 and 7 carry this, and both halves of the performance expectation now reach the learner editions.

Direct assessment: MS-ETS1-1. Task 8 defines the criteria and constraints of the trial with enough precision to guide it, including a damage limit and a stop rule.

Supporting alignment: MS-PS4-1, conditional. Claim this only if your class explicitly models the vibration in terms of amplitude and frequency as wave properties. The packet names both but does not develop a wave model, so do not claim it as written.

Assessment boundary

The packet does not assess wave mathematics, resonance physics, or the biochemistry of pollination. The 124 Hz figure is a measurement recorded for this alien species; students never derive it, never calculate with it, and should never generalise it to Earth flowers. No mathematics standard is claimed.

Science and engineering practices

  • Analyzing and Interpreting Data — Tasks 1 and 3.
  • Engaging in Argument from Evidence — Tasks 5 and 6.
  • Planning and Carrying Out Investigations — Task 4 plans an independent line of evidence; Task 8 designs the monitored trial.
  • Developing and Using Models — the mechanism model in Task 6.
  • Constructing Explanations and Designing Solutions — Tasks 7 and 8.

Crosscutting concepts

Structure and Function

The cone holds pollen until something shakes it. The structure explains why contact is not enough.

Cause and Effect

Three failed trials narrow the cause to one step rather than leaving it open.

Systems and System Models

Sealing the garden removed a partner the plant depends on, without changing the plant.

Stability and Change

Everything stable and normal, and the outcome still changed — because a recurring event stopped.

Source boundary

What each layer covers
LayerWhat belongs to it here
Established Earth sciencePoricidal anthers have pores already present. In buzz pollination a bee grasps the flower and vibrates it mechanically, so pollen leaves through those existing pores. Release depends on frequency, amplitude, duration and coupling rather than one universal frequency. Floral buzzing occurs across several bee taxa, and honeybees do not perform it. Growers assist tomato pollination with commercial vibrating tools, and managed bumblebee colonies are another greenhouse option.
Case-specific evidenceThe lyreflower’s flexible cone, the lyre-moth’s hovering wingbeat, the airborne coupling route, the strongest measured response near 124 Hz, and every garden measurement.
Case inferenceThat the sealed garden’s lack of periodic vibration is why this crop stopped setting fruit. The evidence supports this for this plant in this garden.
Engineering extrapolationThat a calibrated exciter could restore release. This follows from the mechanism, but the outcome still requires a monitored trial.
The two distinctions that carry the case

First: the pores are already present. The pollen is retained, not trapped — nothing opens. Second: the Earth mechanism is a bee gripping a flower, while the case mechanism is airborne oscillation coupling into the tissue. Both shake pollen through existing pores, but they are not the same route and should not be merged.

Claims to correct on sight

  • “The pores open when the right sound arrives.” They are open the whole time. What changes is whether anything shakes the pollen through them.
  • “124 Hz is the magic number.” It is where the strongest release was recorded for this species, and only when amplitude and duration were also sufficient.
  • “Just play a 124 Hz tone in the room.” The vibration has to couple into the cone. Sound filling a room is not the same as vibration delivered to a structure.
  • “Honeybees do this to flowers.” Honeybees do not perform floral buzzing, though they may still visit some of these crops.
  • “Only bumblebees can buzz-pollinate.” Floral buzzing occurs across several bee taxa.
  • “The flower is listening for the moth.” It is mechanical vibration reaching tissue, not hearing.
  • “The researcher was hiding it.” The case states plainly that it is not secrecy. It is a cultural constraint on who may say a thing first.
  • “The plant must be sick.” Every health indicator is normal and the pollen is viable at 98%.

Likely misconceptions

Failure equals no information

Students read three failed trials as a dead end. They are the sharpest evidence in the case: they rule out contact as the missing step.

Something must be blocked

Students look for an obstruction. Nothing is blocked. Return to the shaker.

One number fixes it

124 Hz is memorable and feels like an answer. The four-settings block in Task 8 exists to hold the other three settings in view; it is a labelled comparison rather than a numbered figure.

The researcher is the obstacle

Task 4 reframes this: the constraint is real and the investigation routes around it with independent evidence, which is what a professional would do.

Discussion guidance

  • Ask after Task 1: if everything on the checklist is fine, what kinds of things does a checklist not catch?
  • Ask after Task 2: was anything missing from the shaker? Nothing was. That is the whole idea.
  • Ask after Task 4: when is it right to work around a person rather than press them? This transfers well beyond the case.

Accessibility and differentiation guidance

  • The Accessible Mission runs one task per page, adds a word bank at Task 2, and gives the Claim-Evidence-Reasoning frame a full page of its own.
  • The shaker demonstration is the single highest-value support in this packet. Physical, immediate, and it carries the entire mechanism.
  • Both figures are patterned as well as labelled and carry extended descriptions, so they read in grayscale and aloud.
  • Extension for grades 9–12: ask what measurement would show that the vibration actually reached the cone, rather than merely being produced nearby.

CLAIM

The lyreflower stopped setting fruit because the sealed garden never supplies the vibration its anther cone needs. Pollen stays inside the cone, so no fruit can set and the buds abort.

EVIDENCE

Every condition reads normal: 22.1 °C, 62% humidity, soil pH 6.8, a 14h/10h light cycle and an Earth-normal atmosphere. The pollen is viable at 98% and the stigma is confirmed receptive. The specimen record shows a cone whose pores are already present, packed with mature pollen that gentle contact and a firmer tap both fail to release. Three hand-pollination trials in weeks 3, 4 and 5 placed viable pollen directly on the stigma and none set fruit. The sensor array reports no pollinators, 0.0 m/s airflow, no periodic vibration and a flat 20–200 Hz scan, where comparable Telluvian gardens report signals in the 100–150 Hz range. The database records the strongest release near the lyre-moth's 124 Hz wingbeat when amplitude and duration are also sufficient.

REASONING

Nothing here is absent or broken, which is what makes the case unusual: the pollen exists, the openings exist, and the plant is healthy. In buzz pollination on Earth, pollen leaves a poricidal anther only when a bee vibrates the flower — the pores are already open, and vibration is what moves the grains through them. The three failed trials show that placing pollen on the stigma is not the missing step, because that was done and nothing followed. What the garden lacks is not a substance but an event: the vibration that would shake pollen out of the cone in the first place. This is the best-supported explanation for this plant in this garden. The record does not establish that Earth flowers respond near 124 Hz, and it does not guarantee that an exciter will restore fruit set — that needs a monitored trial.

The setting to vary first: Accept any of the four with a reason. Amplitude is the strongest answer, because the case already reports where the response is strongest but says nothing about how hard the shaking must be. Frequency is acceptable if the student explains they are confirming the reported value rather than discovering it.

What to measure: Pollen actually released — counted, weighed or collected — rather than whether the exciter was switched on. A secure answer measures the outcome, not the intervention.

Damage limit: Any observable harm to the cone, bud or stem: bruising, tearing, wilting, or an increase in bud abortion. This must be stated as an immediate stop, not something to weigh up.

Stop-and-revise rule: A named condition that ends the trial and returns the design for revision — for example, no measurable pollen release after the planned settings have been worked through, or any damage observed at all.

Why 124 Hz alone is not a plan: Frequency is one of four settings that have to be right together. A vibration at exactly 124 Hz that is too weak, too brief, or applied where it never reaches the cone will release nothing. The record reports the strongest response near that value when amplitude and duration are also sufficient, which is a different statement from a guaranteed setting. Full credit requires naming at least two of amplitude, duration and coupling.

Marking note: Keep claims bounded to this species in this garden. Answers that promise recovery, or that extend 124 Hz to Earth flowers, have not met the bounded-claim dimension.
