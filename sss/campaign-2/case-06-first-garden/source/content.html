Student Mission
Mission question

Every soil panel comes back clean, and the garden still grows in thriving patches with failing ground between them. What did the panels never test — and what must an approved trial include before anything living is added to this soil?

What happened

The first Concord agricultural summit on a human world is meeting in a restored terrace garden on Earth. Dr. Nova’s family bought contaminated industrial ground and spent forty years removing toxins and rebuilding topsoil. The chemistry is clean, yet beds three metres apart get the same amendments, water, compost and seed — and some thrive while some fail.

What you have

Five sources. Dr. Nova holds the site history and the fixes already ruled out. Three Concord delegates have come to help: Vorn-Shael has mapped the garden’s chemical signatures, Kess is reconstructing a fragmented ancestral memory of soil biology, and Ilreth-Mar observes for the Biosafety Committee. The Federation Database holds the records and the precedents. No single source names the cause.

Read the example first. Then mark T for each part the record says was tested, and X for the part it says was never tested, and explain why clean panels are not evidence that nothing is wrong.

Think about this first

A report with no line for it

A building inspector checks the wiring, the plumbing, the roof and the foundation. Every item passes, and the report says no problems found. Then the well water turns out to be unsafe. Nothing on the checklist was wrong — there was never a line for the well. A clean report covers the items somebody put on the list, and says nothing about the ones left off it.

This inspection is only an example. It is not a garden record.

Teacher Guide

Purpose

Students diagnose why a restored garden grows in thriving patches with failing ground between them, when forty years of soil chemistry comes back clean. The case turns on the difference between a clean result and a complete one: every panel the restoration ran was honest, and none of them examined the living fungal community. Students then specify the screened, approved, controlled trial that would have to happen before anything living is added to the soil.

The phenomenon

A site survey finds phosphorus, nitrogen, carbon chains and signalling compounds present inside circular patches approximately four to six metres in diameter, and the same compounds at trace levels only between those patches, with sharp boundaries despite adequate matrix diffusion. The beds established from mature garden soil still perform best; the beds built from clean remediated substrate have never matched them, although both receive identical amendments, water, compost and seed. Concord records describe mycorrhizal partnerships in which fungi receive plant carbon and can help hosts acquire phosphorus, nitrogen or water.

Measurable objectives

Separate what a set of tests examined from what it never examined. Describe a spatial pattern in a survey record and state what a chemical survey cannot establish alone. Use fixes that produced no change as evidence against three alternatives. Combine five sources that individually settle nothing. Model a candidate causal pathway and name what the model does not establish. Write an explanation that states its own limits. Specify a trial with identification, screening, approval, untreated controls and monitoring.

Success criteria

Scope is read correctly

Students say that the panels tested chemistry and never tested the soil fungal community, and treat a clean report as evidence about the checked items only.

The mechanism stays a candidate

Missing compatible partners are described as a leading explanation to be tested. No student writes that the fungi are proven absent or that the mechanism is established.

Null results count

The fixes Dr. Nova already ruled out are used as evidence against the alternatives, not skipped as failures.

Convergence is required

The explanation draws on at least three sources, because no single source names the cause.

The trial is safe and testable

The specification names identification, screening, approval, untreated control plots and monitoring, and leaves the choice of organism to the team that screens it.

What is different about this case

This is the sixth Campaign 2 investigation and the only one the game hides until the other five are complete. Two things make it behave differently in a classroom. First, it carries almost no numbers. Cases 01–05 each turned on a measured value checked against a specification; here the only quantities are a patch diameter of approximately four to six metres, roughly three metres between thriving and failing ground, and a set of durations. The reasoning is about scope, elimination and convergence rather than arithmetic. Second, every source is a person or an archive. There is no sensor panel and no specimen examination, so students cannot resolve the case by finding the one reading that is out of range.

The move students find hardest

Students who have played Cases 01–05 arrive expecting a value that is wrong. Nothing here is out of range. The productive question is not “which reading is bad?” but “which question was never asked?” Expect to spend real time on Task 1 before the rest of the packet becomes tractable.

Running the packet

Tasks 1–2 work well immediately after play, while the source conversations are fresh. Task 3 benefits from small-group discussion because the rejections depend on evidence scattered across two sources. Task 4 is the packet’s spine and is worth protecting: students who fill it in honestly write much stronger CERs. Tasks 5–7 can run the following day. The Accessible edition carries one task per page and can be used alongside the Student edition in the same room. The task numbers, the evidence available and the diagnosis are the same in both; the presentation is not. Every record is rewritten in plainer register and shortened, each page carries a sentence frame or a word bank, and the CER keeps a page of its own where the Student edition combines it with Task 7. The evidence tables carry the same numbers in both editions wherever they hold the same record, so a direction naming a table resolves in either hand.

Where the packet stops

The packet never concludes that the fungi are absent, and never concludes that adding fungi would repair the garden. Both would go beyond the evidence. The strongest available conclusion is that failed or incomplete re-establishment of compatible mycorrhizal partners is the best-supported candidate cause of the patch pattern, and that the next step is a screened, approved, controlled trial. Students who want a stronger ending are asking a reasonable question; the answer is that the identification work has not been done yet.

Eight rows record checks the restoration carried out; the ninth records the soil fungal community, which no panel examined. Students who mark the fungal row T have usually read “never tested” as a failed test rather than an absent one. The written prompt is the task’s real work: a clean panel is evidence about the item on the panel and nothing else. The building-inspection example, printed inside Task 1 in both learner editions, exists for students who cannot get there from the garden alone — it deliberately carries no garden values, and its disclaimer line is printed on the page.

Watch for

“The tests were wrong” or “Dr. Nova made a mistake”. Neither is supported. Every test was competently run and correctly reported. The gap is in what the standard panel covers, which is why the case is about scope rather than error.

Complete teaching procedure / task route

Two-period class flow: Period 1 completes Tasks 1–3; Period 2 completes Tasks 4–7. Preserve the registry order and the full graded scope.

  1. Task 1 · Sort What Was Tested from What Was Never Tested — Separate the eight parts of the soil that forty years of restoration examined from the one part no panel ever examined, and explain why a set of clean results is not evidence that nothing is wrong.
  2. Task 2 · Read the Pattern in the Site Survey — Describe what the chemical survey found inside a surveyed patch and what it found between patches, and explain why a chemical survey cannot identify the organism or prove an absence on its own.
  3. Task 3 · Weigh the Explanations — Use the construction history of the two kinds of bed to select the best-supported candidate cause, and write the record that rules out each of the three rejected explanations.
  4. Task 4 · Show Where the Five Sources Converge — State what each of the five sources establishes and what it cannot establish alone, then write one sentence for what the five together support and what they still leave untested.
  5. Task 5 · Model the Candidate Pathway — Complete the condition-mechanism-effect model in causal order using the word bank, then name one thing the completed model does not establish.
  6. Task 6 · Explain the Diagnosis with CER — Write a Claim-Evidence-Reasoning explanation using evidence from more than one source, keeping the candidate cause framed as a candidate, and naming at least one thing the evidence does not establish.
  7. Task 7 · Specify the Screened, Approved Trial — Define what the trial must compare and why it needs untreated control plots, name one constraint, and justify why approval and screening still apply to a transfer in which the fungi and the soil both come from Earth.

Table 3 supplies the piece students need and the earlier tasks do not: the two kinds of bed differ in biological history, not in chemistry or care. The original beds were established from mature garden soil; the expansion beds were built from clean remediated substrate. Everything applied since has been identical. That contrast is what makes a biological explanation worth testing.

The three rejections each rest on a specific record. Concrete leaching and pH: the pH was corrected years ago and has been verified since, so a pH explanation contradicts a measurement that was actually taken. Irrigation: the survey finds the compounds present everywhere and immobile between zones, and irrigation uniformity was verified — the pattern is about distribution, not supply. Invasive organisms from the delegates’ ships: the biosafety protocols were followed strictly, and the patchiness predates the summit by decades. That last one is worth naming out loud as the politically convenient answer, which is a habit of mind the case is training against.

No source resolves the case alone, and the table is where students discover that. Dr. Nova establishes the site history and eliminates the conventional fixes, but measures nothing about the living community. Delegate Vorn-Shael establishes the chemical distribution, but is a chemist and says so. Delegate Kess supplies the biological mechanism from a fragmented ancestral memory and states that the garden’s own organisms still have to be identified. Delegate Ilreth-Mar establishes the regulatory constraint but contributes no biology. The Federation Database supplies the Earth records and the exemption precedent, and explicitly declines to generalise. Accept any limit statement that correctly identifies what the source does not measure.

Stages 1 and 5 are fixed: the beds’ construction history at one end, the surveyed pattern at the other. Stages 2–4 carry the candidate mechanism in causal order — compatible fungi may not have re-established in the beds built from clean substrate; fewer roots there form partnerships whose hyphae extend into the surrounding soil; less of the resource acquisition those partnerships contribute happens in that ground. The word bank is exact-match, so mis-ordering is visible immediately.

The limit response is not optional

A completed pathway looks like a proof, and students read it that way unless asked not to. The final prompt requires them to name something the model does not establish. Strong answers include: which fungi are actually present in either kind of bed; whether the fungi in the original beds would colonise the expansion beds; whether anything else changed when the expansion beds were built; and whether adding fungi would improve the garden.

The Accessible CER occupies a full page of its own. In the Student edition it shares its page with Task 7 under the approved combined contract, because on a page of its own it left roughly half the page empty. A complete claim names the candidate cause and says that it is a candidate. Evidence should draw on at least three sources and must include at least one item the alternatives cannot accommodate — most often the construction history of the two bed types, or the sharp boundary. Reasoning should connect the biological history of the beds to the surveyed distribution, and must name a limit. A CER that reads as a settled finding is the failure mode to correct, even when every individual sentence is accurate.

Two prompts do most of the work. The control-plot prompt asks why untreated plots are necessary when the treated plots are the ones being tested; the answer is that without them a change cannot be attributed to the treatment rather than to weather, season, or the garden’s own slow recovery. The approval prompt asks why review is required when both the fungi and the soil are from Earth. Students often assume that within-world means risk-free. It does not: transferred material can carry pathogens or organisms that spread beyond where they were put, and an unreviewed transfer would also invalidate the evidence even if a plot improved.

Science boundary

Established Earth science. Many land plants associate with mycorrhizal fungi. Hyphae extend beyond the root, receive carbon from the plant, and can help particular hosts acquire phosphorus, nitrogen or water. A single fungal individual may colonise more than one root, which is what a common mycorrhizal network is. Experiments sometimes detect movement of carbon, nutrients, water or signalling compounds along such shared pathways, but the amount, the direction, and whether a receiving plant benefits vary with the fungus, the plant, the soil and the experimental design. Industrial disturbance can damage soil fungal communities as well as soil chemistry.

Case-specific evidence. The garden, the forty-year restoration, the two kinds of bed, the surveyed chemical distribution, the patch dimensions, the delegates, the biosafety regulation and the exemption precedents are records made for this case.

Modelled or estimated evidence. The global estimates of hyphal length in topsoil and of annual plant-carbon allocation to mycorrhizal mycelium are published global estimates, not measurements of this garden. The carbon figure is an annual flux, not a quantity of stored carbon. Neither figure is used anywhere in the student-facing tasks.

What the packet may conclude. That the soil fungal community was never examined; that the surveyed compounds are abundant inside patches and at trace levels between them with sharp boundaries; that the two kinds of bed differ in biological history and not in chemistry or care; that three alternative explanations are ruled out by records already taken; and that failed or incomplete re-establishment of compatible mycorrhizal partners is therefore the best-supported candidate cause, to be tested by a screened, approved, controlled trial.

Claims to correct on sight

These are quoted here so that you can recognise them. None of them is supported by this case, and none should survive in student work:

  • “The soil is a wood wide web — one big cooperative internet linking every plant in the garden.”
  • “Mother trees feed their own seedlings through the network and send them more than strangers.”
  • “The forest is a single superorganism, so the garden is one organism too.”
  • “The network was never restored, so the compatible fungi are definitely gone.”
  • “It is Earth fungi going into Earth soil, so there is no risk and no approval is needed.”
  • “Adding fungi will fix the garden. Inoculation is a guaranteed cure.”

Figures and tables

Standards

MS-ETS1-1 is the direct alignment, and Task 7 is where it is assessed: students write the two criteria the trial must meet — what it must compare, and why untreated control plots are necessary even though the treated plots are the ones being tested — and name one constraint it must work within, against a record that specifies organism identification, pathogen and invasion screening, prior approval under Section 14.7, replicated untreated controls, and scheduled monitoring. Supporting only: MS-LS2-3, at Task 5, where students order a pathway in which fungi receive plant carbon and can improve a host’s acquisition of phosphorus, nitrogen or water. It is supporting and not direct because the case supplies no cycling or energy-flow measurement at all; the model orders a candidate mechanism rather than describing a quantified flow. MS-LS2-2 is not claimed: its performance expectation predicts patterns of interactions across multiple ecosystems, and this packet holds one restored terrace with two bed histories. MS-ETS1-2 is not claimed: Task 3 weighs competing explanations, not competing design solutions against shared criteria. No mathematics standard is claimed; the case contains no calculation.

CLAIM

Failed or incomplete re-establishment of compatible mycorrhizal partners in the beds built from clean remediated substrate is the best-supported candidate cause of the garden’s patch pattern. It has not been established, and testing it is the next step.

EVIDENCE

Forty years of soil work examined pH, minerals, drainage, toxins, organic matter, irrigation, pests and nutrients, and the soil fungal community was never tested. The site survey finds phosphorus, nitrogen, carbon chains and signalling compounds present inside circular patches of approximately four to six metres and at trace levels only between them, with sharp boundaries despite adequate matrix diffusion. The original beds were established from mature garden soil and still perform best; the expansion beds were built from clean remediated substrate and have never matched them, although both receive identical amendments, water, compost and seed. Concord records describe fungi that colonise compatible roots and can help hosts acquire phosphorus, nitrogen or water.

REASONING

The two kinds of bed differ in biological history and in nothing else on the record, which points the explanation at the living community rather than at chemistry or care. A distribution that stays compartmentalised where the matrix would allow diffusion is consistent with reduced biological transport, and the three alternatives are each contradicted by a measurement already taken. This evidence does not establish which fungi are present in either kind of bed, does not establish that compatible partners are absent, and does not establish that adding fungi would repair the garden — which is why the next step is a screened, approved, controlled trial rather than a treatment.

  • Criterion 1 — compare replicated treated plots against untreated control plots, measuring colonisation and plant performance on a set schedule. That comparison is the one that separates an effect of the treatment from anything else changing in the garden.
  • Criterion 2 — without untreated controls, an improvement could follow the season, the weather, or the garden’s own slow recovery. A treated plot that improves proves nothing on its own.
  • Constraint — accept any of: prior Concord approval is required under Section 14.7; candidates must pass pathogen and invasion screening; provenance must be recorded; host compatibility must be checked; this is a summit site under observation; the garden is three generations of a family’s work.
  • Why approval and screening still apply within Earth — transferred living material can carry pathogens or organisms that spread beyond where they are placed, and effects have been observed beyond directly treated plants. An unreviewed transfer would also invalidate the evidence even if a plot improved, and would breach Section 14.7.
