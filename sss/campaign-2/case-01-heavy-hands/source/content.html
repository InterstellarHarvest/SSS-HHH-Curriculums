Mission question

The bed measures exactly 2.10 g where it was calibrated. So why are the gorlroot tubers buckling sideways, and what did the specification leave out?

Case briefing

A Vressk agricultural ring orbiting Kepler-442b spins to grow gorlroot, a staple tuber from a 2.1 g world. The primary roots point outward toward the ring wall, the correct down direction here, but the tubers buckle sideways as they swell and push through the soil surface. The botanist calibrated the midpoint to 2.10 g and cannot explain it.

Reasoning rule

Two things can both be true: a measurement can be correct, and it can still leave something out. Watch for that. Copy any value exactly as it is written, and remember that what gorlroot does is something this investigation recorded — not a rule about plants on Earth.

Case closed — what it means

The habitat passed its test. The crop still failed. Nobody was careless: the botanist measured the value the specification named, and measured it correctly. What was incomplete was the requirement itself, because it named one place and said nothing about the rest. Any time a rule tells you to measure in one spot, the useful question is what that spot is not telling you.

Teacher Guide

Lesson overview

Students investigate why gorlroot tubers buckle sideways in a Vressk centrifuge habitat whose soil bed measures exactly 2.10 g at the point where it was calibrated. Soil and seed stock were each changed across three plantings without effect; nutrients, light and water are reported at present values that were never varied, so no planting has tested them. The investigation separates the magnitude of an acceleration from its direction, applies a = ω²r across the depth of a growing bed, and ends with the specification clause the original build never contained.

The packet is built for grades 6–8 and adapts upward for grades 9–12. It runs with or without gameplay: the essential evidence ledger on page 4 lets a class complete every task from the printed evidence alone.

Learning target

I can use evidence from five sources to explain why an environment that passed its written test still failed an organism, and I can write the requirement that test was missing.

Measurable objectives

  • Distinguish how strong a pull is from which way it points.
  • Explain why a measurement can be correct and still not answer the question that matters.
  • Separate a condition the record actually changed and tested from a condition reported at a present value, and say why the second has not been ruled out.
  • Copy the values the packet prints — 2.10 g, ±0.05 g, 20 cm, 600 m and the recorded onset days — exactly as written, and add no quantity the case does not report.
  • Use a size-dependent pattern to discriminate among candidate causes.
  • Combine evidence from all five sources and state what each cannot establish alone.
  • Reject the over-calibration, nutrient and bed-depth alternatives with evidence.
  • Write an across-bed criterion, compare two proposals against it, and define a monitored trial with a stop rule.
  • Distinguish established physics from the measurements and the growth response recorded in this case.

Success criteria

Direction is not confused with magnitude

Answers say the magnitude changes across the bed and the direction stays outward. Nothing describes down as tilting, rotating or reversing.

Data are exact

The printed values — 2.10 g, ±0.05 g, 20 cm, 600 m, the onset days — are copied as written, never rounded or rewritten. No deformation quantity and no figure for the difference across the bed is invented, because the packet reports neither.

Evidence converges

The explanation uses more than one source and rests on the size pattern as well as on what the sensor array found in three places.

Design is testable

The criterion names where and across what span it applies, and the trial has a measurement schedule and a stop rule.

Standards alignment and limitation

Direct assessment: MS-LS1-5. The genetic factor is varied in the printed record and the environmental one is not: Table 1 shows Crop 3 planted from new seed stock with the deformation returning on day 11, and Table 2 asks students to record that the ring radius and rotation rate were never changed. Task 7 then requires an evidence-based explanation of the growth outcome that uses both. Students see the genetic change and the unchanged rotational environment on the page, so the environmental attribution is assessed rather than assumed.

Direct assessment: MS-ETS1-1. Task 8 asks students to write the across-bed criterion the original specification omitted and to name a constraint reported in the case. Both responses are on the page — the criterion and the constraint — and Table 7 supplies the reported constraints (engineering work for the larger ring; immediate trial for the thinner bed).

Supporting alignment: MS-ETS1-2, conditional. Claim this only if your class systematically compares the larger ring against the thinner bed using the same criterion and the same constraint. Table 7 sets the comparison up; the packet as written does not force it to be carried out systematically. If you do not run that comparison, do not claim it.

MS-ETS1-3 is not claimed

An earlier version of this packet claimed MS-ETS1-3 on the strength of the two Concord centrifuge records. It is withdrawn. That expectation asks students to analyse data from several design solutions and combine the best characteristics into a new one. No task here does that: the Concord records are Teacher-facing, learners never see two design outcomes to analyse, and Task 8 compares two proposals against a criterion rather than combining them. No standard has been substituted for it.

Assessment boundary

Do not claim assessment of rotating-reference-frame physics, Newton’s laws, or the derivation of a = ω²r. Students never meet the formula. They meet the relationship it describes through the merry-go-round in Task 2, and the packet requires no calculation anywhere. Do not claim a mathematics standard from this packet. The packet also does not assess plant cell structure or the biological mechanism of gravity sensing; the gorlroot growth response is treated as evidence recorded in this case.

Why Task 2 uses invented numbers

The habitat’s own readings differ by less than a tenth of one percent, because a 20 cm bed sits inside a 224.9 m radius. That is not a flaw in the case: a ring with a classroom-friendly spread would need a radius of about two metres — roughly four metres across — and would have to spin near 30 RPM, which no habitat could use. So Task 2 teaches the relationship with a merry-go-round and three invented values, clearly labelled on the page as not being measurements from the habitat. The real readings then appear as reference evidence in the case file, where students use them to choose a diagnosis rather than to do arithmetic.

Science and engineering practices

  • Analyzing and Interpreting Data — Tasks 2, 3 and 4.
  • Planning and Carrying Out Investigations — Task 3 examines why one measurement was not enough, and Task 8 designs the monitored trial.
  • Developing and Using Models — the mechanism model in Task 6.
  • Engaging in Argument from Evidence — Tasks 5 and 6.
  • Constructing Explanations and Designing Solutions — Tasks 7 and 8.

Crosscutting concepts

Scale, Proportion, and Quantity

A difference far too small to notice is still decisive for one organism. The merry-go-round in Task 2 carries that idea without asking students to handle the decimals.

Cause and Effect

The size-dependent pattern is what separates a mechanism from a coincidence in time.

Systems and System Models

The habitat is a system whose specification bounded one point rather than the whole bed.

Structure and Function

An organ that spans more of the bed samples more of the difference, so structure determines exposure.

Source boundary

Four layers are kept separate throughout the packet. Naming them for students is the fastest way to stop the case being over-generalised.

What each layer covers
LayerWhat belongs to it here
Established Earth scienceIn a rotating frame the magnitude of the apparent acceleration follows a = ω²r, so at one rotation rate it grows with distance from the axis. For a chosen target magnitude, a larger radius allows a lower rotation rate. Earth plants orient growth using gravity-sensing tissue, including statolith-bearing cells.
Case-specific evidenceThe radius, the rotation rate, the bed depth, the three reported magnitudes and their difference, the size-dependent deformation, the Vressk cultivation records, and the two Concord centrifuge outcomes.
Case inferenceThat the gorlroot growth-control system responds to the across-bed difference by growing the two sides of a tuber unequally. The evidence supports this for this crop in this habitat.
Engineering extrapolationThat a larger radius or a thinner bed would reduce the difference enough to prevent the deformation. This follows from a = ω²r for the geometry, but the biological outcome still requires a monitored trial.
The distinction that carries the case

The reported direction is outward at every sampled radius. What changes across the bed is the magnitude. If a student describes the field as tilting, rotating or reversing, correct it immediately — the whole diagnosis depends on the difference being one of size, not of direction.

Claims to correct on sight

  • “Down points different ways at the top and the bottom of the bed.” The profile reports outward at all three radii. Only the magnitude differs.
  • “The centrifuge is spinning too fast, so the crop is above 2.1 g.” The midpoint is calibrated to 2.10 g and the specification allows ±0.05 g. Both endpoint values sit inside that band.
  • “The tubers are growing upward.” They curve sideways across the row, neither toward the axis nor away from it.
  • “Earth plants would notice a difference this small too.” Nothing in the record establishes that. The response belongs to this crop in this habitat.
  • “The Coriolis effect is doing this.” No Coriolis measurement appears anywhere in the evidence. Introducing it replaces the reported cause with an unreported one.
  • “The two difference numbers disagree, so one is wrong.” 0.0018 g is what you get by subtracting two values already rounded to four decimals. 0.00187 g is reported directly and therefore keeps more digits. Both are consistent.
  • “Negligible means zero.” It is the botanist, not the archive, who calls the bed-scale difference negligible — first as a judgement about a 20 cm bed, then as a statement that the difference is negligible for cultivation on Vress. That is a person’s assessment, not an archived measurement. Neither statement reports it as zero, and no planetary figure is given.
  • “A bigger ring will fix it.” It reduces the difference, which is why record GC-1208 was resolved that way. Whether this crop recovers is a question for a monitored trial.
  • “Nutrients, light and water were checked, so they are ruled out.” They were reported at a present value, not tested. Only soil and seed stock were changed between plantings, so nothing in this record eliminates the other conditions.
  • “The ring status is nominal, so there is no vibration and no wobble.” The sensor array reports only Ring Status: NOMINAL. The botanist is the one who reports steady RPM with no vibration and no wobble. A nominal status is not a report about every mechanical condition that was not mentioned.
  • “A 300 m ring is safe, because GC-1445 reported no misalignment at 300 m.” That record is an Oolian kelp crop of low gravitropic precision, and the same database concludes that gradient sensitivity is species-dependent. It establishes nothing about a safe radius, and nothing about gorlroot.

Optional extension · precision

The array reports the difference directly as 0.00187 g. A student who subtracts the two endpoint values instead gets 0.0018 g, and may ask which is right. Both are. 2.0991 g and 2.1009 g were already rounded to four decimals, and subtracting two rounded values cannot recover precision the rounding removed, so that result can only be trusted to four decimals. The array reports its difference without going through that rounding, so it keeps a fifth digit. This is worth ten minutes with older learners who are working from this guide’s numerical ledger, and it is deliberately not a task: the endpoint values are printed nowhere in either learner edition, so no student can be asked to produce or explain the subtraction. It is also interesting enough to displace the biology if it becomes one.

Data-use cautions

  • Direction is reported as outward at every sampled radius. Never let it be redrawn as a change of direction.
  • Preserve the reported precision in both directions: do not extend 2.10 g to more decimals, and do not shorten 0.00187 g.
  • Do not treat 0.0018 g and 0.00187 g as conflicting measurements.
  • Do not ask for a value in radians per second. The case reports the rotation rate in RPM only, and no task requires the conversion.
  • Do not invent a deformation quantity. Only the direction of the trend is reported.
  • Do not attach a number to the planetary bed-scale difference. None is reported.

Likely misconceptions

Magnitude read as direction

Students picture the bed top and base pulling different ways. Return to the analogy: every rider is thrown outward, and only the strength changes.

Small therefore irrelevant

The difference is real but very small. That is the point, not a flaw: it was small enough that nobody wrote a rule about it, and one crop still noticed.

Someone must have been careless

Students look for a person to blame. The point of the case is that every person did their job and the requirement itself was incomplete.

Generalising to Earth crops

Students conclude that plants sense gravity gradients. The record supports a statement about this crop in this habitat and nothing wider.

Discussion guidance

  • Ask early: is the botanist wrong about the 2.10 g reading? The answer is no, and holding that steady is what makes the case work.
  • Ask after Task 4: which alternatives could still be true if tubers of every size deformed equally? This is where the size pattern earns its place.
  • Ask after Task 8: what would have to be written into the next specification for this to be caught before planting?

Accessibility and differentiation guidance

  • The Accessible Mission runs one task per page, adds a word bank at Task 3, and gives the Claim-Evidence-Reasoning frame a full page of its own with six sentence starters and a pointer to the pages that hold the evidence.
  • Every figure is patterned as well as labelled and carries an extended description, so it reads in grayscale and aloud.
  • For students who stall on Task 3, give the ring as a bicycle wheel: every spoke turns together, so a point near the rim travels further in the same time than a point near the hub.
  • Extension for grades 9–12: ask what tolerance the specification should carry, and what measurement would be needed to justify the number chosen.

Quick grading

If you need one number quickly: Task 6 for the diagnosis and rejections, Task 7 for the written explanation. Together they carry the case. Tasks 7 and 8 are also written to be completed without a partner and without replaying the case; for a single independent grade, use Task 7 with the formal rubric on the next page and Task 8 as a transfer check.

Annotated answers and grading guidance

  • Task 2. Purpose: establish that the pull grows with distance from the centre, and that something long enough to span positions feels more than one value at once. CLAIM

At one rotation rate the outward acceleration magnitude increases across the radial depth of the soil bed, and the gorlroot growth-control system responds to that difference across a swelling tuber by growing its two sides unequally, which curves the tuber sideways.

EVIDENCE

The centrifuge sensor array measured in three places instead of one and found the pull weakest at the top of the 20 cm bed, exactly right in the middle, and strongest at the bottom, with everything pulled the same way — outward. The specimen record reports that deformation increases with tuber diameter and that the curve runs sideways across the row, neither toward the axis nor away from it. Soil was reformulated for Crop 2 and new seed stock used for Crop 3, and the deformation still appeared, on days 10 and 11. The conditions the botanist reports now — nutrients, light and water — were never changed between plantings, so nothing in the record has tested them. The habitat rule book named a value for the middle of the bed and said nothing about the rest of it.

REASONING

The ring turns as one piece, so a place farther from the centre is pulled harder — that is what the merry-go-round shows and what the sensor array found across the bed. The base of the bed sits farther out than the top, so it must be pulled harder even when the midpoint is calibrated exactly, and a reading taken at one point cannot detect that. A thicker tuber reaches across more of the bed, so it sits across more of the difference — which is why the deformation grows with diameter rather than appearing equally at every size. That size dependence is what makes a uniform cause such as nutrients or bed depth a poor fit. This is the best-supported explanation for this crop in this habitat; the record does not establish that any other organism responds to a difference of this size, it does not rule out the conditions that were never tested, and the proposed remedies still require a monitored trial.

Across-bed criterion the specification left out: A maximum permitted difference in magnitude across the full radial depth of the growing bed, measured at the bed top and the bed base rather than at one point. A secure answer names where the requirement applies and over what span. Accept any tolerance value provided the student says the number itself would have to be justified by a trial.

One constraint reported in this case: A larger ring requires engineering work, while a thinner bed can be trialled immediately. Accept also the fixed 2.1 g target.

Table 7 — does each proposal meet the criterion: Larger radius: yes. Spinning a longer ring more slowly keeps the pull at the same target, and the same 20 cm of bed depth is then a smaller share of a longer radius, so the difference across the bed falls. Record GC-1208 resolved the matching problem this way, by extending the radius to 600 m. Thinner bed: yes, by the other route — a shallower bed reaches across less of the difference, so each plant samples less of it. Neither answer may promise recovery; both proposals still need the monitored trial.

Monitored trial and stop rule: A secure answer measures the magnitude at the bed top and the bed base rather than at the midpoint alone, records tuber diameter alongside deformation on a stated schedule, and runs alongside an unchanged control planting. The stop rule should name an observation that ends the trial — for example, deformation still increasing with diameter by the day it first appeared in earlier crops — and should send the design back for revision rather than continuing to full scale.
