Mission question

What caused this spirulina reactor’s repeating crash-and-recovery cycle, and how should its light controls change to prevent another crash?

Case briefing

A spirulina photobioreactor ran reliably for four months. After the habitat lighting schedule changed, the culture began crashing, rebuilding, and crashing again. Determine which change matters and explain the mechanism.

Analysis rule

A timeline can identify a possible cause. To establish a mechanism, connect the timing to observations and reactor operating information.

Teacher Guide · Science qualifications and misconceptions
SolarAgriculturalAgency

Science qualification

Commercially called spirulina is a photosynthetic cyanobacterium, not a plant. Current sources may use Arthrospira or Limnospira. This taxonomy is teacher background, not a Student task.

More light is not automatically better. When absorbed energy exceeds the culture’s capacity to use or dissipate it, photosynthetic systems can become inhibited and reactive oxygen chemistry can damage pigments, proteins, and membranes. Risk depends on light intensity and duration as well as mixing, culture density, temperature, nutrients, and dilution.

This fictional reactor was stable under its validated 16/8 schedule. Uncontrolled 24/0 corridor exposure raised its daily light dose beyond the current configuration’s safe range. The lesson does not establish that every spirulina culture needs night.

Misconception responses

Misconceptions and concise teacher responses
If students say…Respond with…
“Spirulina is a plant.”It is a photosynthetic cyanobacterium grown as a culture in this photobioreactor.
“All spirulina need darkness.”This configuration needs its validated recovery interval unless independent controls establish other safe conditions.
“Continuous cultivation cannot work.”It can work in appropriately controlled systems; this reactor lacked those controls.
“The timeline proves the cause.”The timing identifies a candidate. Microscopy and the manual support the mechanism.
“Low carbon dioxide caused the crash.”In this case, damaged cells first reduce uptake; the gas change follows the damage.
Language to avoid

Avoid universal-night claims, claims that continuous cultivation is impossible, plant terminology for spirulina, and explanations based on “dark reactions.”

CLAIM
Uncontrolled 24/0 corridor lighting exceeded this spirulina photobioreactor’s validated operating range and caused its repeating crash-and-recovery cycle.
EVIDENCE
The reactor was stable for four months on 16/8. The first crash occurred about one week after the switch to uncontrolled 24/0, then crashes repeated every 6–8 days. Microscopy showed photooxidative damage, and the manual warned this configuration against uncontrolled 24/0 exposure.
REASONING
The schedule change increased daily light dose under unchanged reactor conditions. Absorbed light exceeded safe processing capacity, so photodamage outpaced repair and productivity and gas exchange fell. Survivors rebuilt, but unchanged exposure drove the next crash.

Immediate: Restore the validated dark or low-light recovery interval.

Longer term: Add independent intensity and schedule control, monitor actual exposure and culture response, and validate settings for the current mixing and density. Continuous cultivation remains possible under appropriately controlled conditions.

Effect. Carbon dioxide uptake and oxygen production fall after light-driven cell damage reduces photosynthetic performance. They are indicators of the crash, not the initiating change in this case.
