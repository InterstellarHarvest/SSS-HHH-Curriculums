# Formal Lesson Plan — Lunar Greenhouse

**Campaign:** 1  
**Case:** 02  
**Student identity:** Process Modeler  
**Duration:** One class period, up to 60 minutes  
**Status:** VALIDATION BUILD

## Overview

Students investigate healthy tomato plants that flower but do not set fruit in a sealed lunar greenhouse. They use case evidence to construct a reproductive-process model, identify the first failed step, evaluate competing explanations, write a compact CER, and propose a support method that restores the missing action.

## Audience and course context

Designed for middle school through adult/general audiences in environmental science, life science, agriculture, or STEM. The lesson assumes only a basic understanding that flowering plants reproduce and that fruit contains seeds.

## Standards alignment

This lesson **supports components of NGSS MS-LS1-4** rather than claiming full independent fulfillment.

- **SEP:** Engaging in Argument from Evidence through the diagnosis, alternative rejection, and CER; Developing and Using Models through the process sequence.
- **DCI:** LS1.B — plants reproduce in varied ways and reproductive success may depend on specialized structures and external actions.
- **CCC:** Cause and Effect; Structure and Function.
- **Assessed product:** A completed process model, failed-step analysis, evidence-based diagnosis, CER, and constrained engineering response.
- **Alignment limit:** The case centers on a self-pollinating tomato flower in a designed habitat; it does not survey the full diversity of plant reproductive strategies.

## Measurable objectives

By the end of the lesson, each student will be able to:

1. Put six major pollination-to-fruit-set events in a scientifically defensible sequence.
2. Identify the first failed step in the lunar greenhouse and support it with at least two observations.
3. Reject at least one plausible alternative using case evidence.
4. Explain the diagnosis in a concise Claim-Evidence-Reasoning response.
5. Propose a pollination support with a criterion, a constraint, and a mechanism-based success check.

## Success criteria

A secure response:

- distinguishes pollination from fertilization and fruit set;
- identifies missing physical agitation/pollen release as the first case failure;
- uses undisturbed pollen plus at least one independent source;
- explains why healthy vegetative growth does not prove successful reproduction;
- proposes a support method that restores pollen release/transfer and can be verified.

## Vocabulary

Anther, fertilization, fruit set, pollen tube, pollination, stigma.

## Prerequisite knowledge

Students should know that flowers are reproductive structures and that fruits develop after successful reproductive events. No prior knowledge of greenhouse tomato production is required.

## Lesson procedure

### 0–5 minutes — Setup

Students open the game and packet. Explain that the academic product is the model and reasoning, not the game score.

### 5–10 minutes — Launch

State the phenomenon: “The plants are green and flowering, but no tomatoes form.” Students complete **2 · Initial thinking**. Do not mention bees, wind, or vibration yet.

### 10–28 minutes — Investigation

Students obtain one formal clue from each source:

- Crew: healthy flowers drop without fruit.
- Sensors: air movement is nearly absent while major growth conditions are nominal.
- Plants: mature pollen remains undisturbed on anthers; stigmas are clean.
- Logs: the pollination strategy was left unresolved and no system was installed.

Prompt students to record only evidence that helps locate a step in the process. Optional stem-shaking or hand/mechanical-pollination nodes may be used as a mechanism test, not as a required bonus route.

### 28–38 minutes — Evidence processing

Students complete **3 · Model the pollination sequence** and **4 · Identify the failed step**. Check that students mark physical agitation/pollen release before pollen reaches the stigma. Ask: “Which observed event is the last step that is definitely working?”

### 38–48 minutes — Diagnosis and explanation

Students submit the game diagnosis, then complete **5 · Test the competing explanations**, **6 · Diagnose and reject an alternative**, and **7 · Claim-Evidence-Reasoning**. Require evidence from more than one source when possible.

### 48–55 minutes — Application

Students complete **8 · Design a reliable pollination support**. Accept manual, mechanical, or carefully controlled airflow approaches when the response explains how flower clusters are agitated and includes a criterion, constraint, and verification method.

### 55–60 minutes — Exit

Students complete **9 · Exit ticket** independently. Collect the packet or save the fillable HTML locally.

## Checks for understanding

- After gameplay: “What is the last confirmed working step?”
- During the model: “Is pollination the same event as fertilization?”
- Before CER: “Which evidence rules out a general growth failure?”
- During design: “What observable result proves the missing step was restored?”

## Assessment

Use the quick rubric for ordinary feedback or the formal analytic rubric for portfolio/graduate-program evidence. Do not grade game score, speed, rank, rapport, or optional exploration.

## Differentiation and accessibility

- Use the Accessible edition’s linear sequence, larger type, and larger response areas.
- Permit bullet-point evidence before prose reasoning.
- Read directions aloud or use text-to-speech; all essential information is real text.
- Provide the fallback evidence digest if navigation, reading load, or technology blocks access.
- Let students complete all work independently; discussion is optional.
- For advanced students, ask how temperature or humidity could produce a similar symptom at a different process step.

## Misconceptions to prevent

1. **Self-pollinating means no movement is needed.** Tomato flowers can contain both reproductive structures, but pollen still must be released and reach a receptive stigma.
2. **Pollination equals fertilization.** Pollination is pollen transfer; fertilization occurs later after pollen-tube growth.
3. **No bees means no plant reproduction anywhere.** Different plant species use different structures and vectors; this case concerns tomato flowers in a sealed habitat.
4. **Healthy leaves prove the reproductive system is healthy.** Vegetative growth and successful fruit set are related but separable processes.
5. **A single numeric airflow target is universally correct.** The game’s sensor and equipment numbers are case values, not universal engineering standards.

## Science and fiction boundaries

- **Established:** tomato flowers produce pollen in anthers; pollen transfer to a receptive stigma is followed by germination, pollen-tube growth, fertilization, and fruit development; flower agitation can support greenhouse tomato pollination.
- **Plausible extrapolation:** a sealed off-world greenhouse would need an intentional pollination strategy and verification process.
- **Fictional:** Shackleton greenhouse personnel, exact readings, design records, and equipment omissions.
- **Do not generalize:** the case does not prove that every crop requires bees, wind, or the same vibration method.

## Real-world relevance

Controlled-environment agriculture requires designers to preserve biological processes that outdoor ecosystems often supply without being noticed. The case is a systems-engineering lesson: an otherwise successful habitat can fail because one reproductive operation was omitted.
