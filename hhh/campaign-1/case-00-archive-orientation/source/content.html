Teacher Guide

Launch sheet

This is the program opener. It teaches how records are handled, not a period of history. Nothing here depends on students remembering historical content, and no later case depends on students having played the orientation level.

Before class

Print the four-page Student Mission, or the four-page Accessible Mission. Page 2 carries the complete source dossier, so the packet is fully teachable with no device in the room. Decide before class which route you are running.

Central resolution

An archive preserves claims. Preservation is a statement about custody, not about truth. A record's weight comes from its provenance, its kind, and whether an independent source corroborates it.

The two routes

Either route assesses the same six tasks
RouteWhat students doWhat you must still supply
Game routePlay the orientation level: speak with the two handlers, read the archive entries, scan the resonance map, then initialize the thread.Nothing extra. The dossier on Student page 2 duplicates the four sources, so absent students are not disadvantaged.
No-game routeRead the dossier on Student page 2 and work the same six tasks.Two minutes of framing. Read the Fictional Context box on page 1 aloud.

Essential evidence

Four sources of three different kinds: two conversations, one archive record, one instrument output. The three kinds must not receive the same limit in 3 · Sort the Orientation Sources by Status. That contrast is the point of the task.

Likely sticking point

Students treat the archive record as automatically the strongest source because it looks official. Redirect: an archive entry establishes that a claim was recorded and kept, which is different from establishing that the claim is correct.

What to collect

Collect the whole packet. 5 · Write the Archive Procedure is the graded culminating product; 6 · Verify a Newly Discovered Record is the transfer check.

Teacher framing line

“Everything about the year 2387 in this program is made up. The way these archivists handle a record is not. By the end of the period you will have written that procedure yourself, and you will use it on every case after this one.”

Classroom and technical fallback

If devices fail mid-period, switch to the no-game route and change nothing else. If the period is cut short, protect 5 · Write the Archive Procedure and 6 · Verify a Newly Discovered Record; 2 · Trace a Record from Accession to Interpretation can be finished as homework. Dropping 3 · Sort the Orientation Sources by Status removes the contribution-and-limitation evidence from the rubric — this is a modified assessment route, and the source-status criterion can no longer be scored.

Lesson overview

One class period. Students learn six archival terms, trace a record from creation to interpretation, sort four fictional sources by what each can and cannot establish, separate the invented setting from the transferable practice, and then write the archive procedure in their own words. This is an orientation, not a historical case: there is no diagnosis and no historical claim to defend.

Guiding historical question

An archive has kept a record safe for a very long time. What does that prove about the record — and what does it still not prove?

Standards alignment and claim limits

Task-first alignment; every claim is scoped to what the tasks actually measure
StandardStatusWhere it is measured, and the limit

CCSS RH.6-8.1Directly assessedTask 3 requires citing what a specific source states before judging it. Limit: the cited texts are invented and are not primary sources.

NGSSNot claimedThis unit assesses historical source handling. It does not assess a science or engineering practice, and no NGSS alignment is asserted.

Measurable objectives

  1. Place all six archival terms correctly from an exact-match bank.
  2. Complete the accession and transmission links of a provenance chain and name one link where unearned weight enters.
  3. Give each of three source kinds a limit appropriate to that kind, rather than one generic limit.
  4. Name the four invented elements of the setting and distinguish them from two transferable practices.
  5. Write an ordered archive procedure using at least four of the six terms and ending in a weight decision.
  6. Name two verification checks for an unfamiliar record and justify each.

Success criteria

Students can say what an archive guarantees and what it does not; give a testimony source, a record source, and an instrument source three different limits; and end a source judgment with how much weight a claim can carry rather than with a verdict on its truth.

Academic vocabulary

Six terms, alphabetical. Students meet them in Task 1 and use them through Task 5.

Materials, technology, and preparation

One Student or Accessible packet per learner. Devices are optional. On the game route the orientation level takes roughly ten minutes and ends when students initialize the thread. No preparation beyond printing is required.

Complete teaching procedure / task route

One ordinary class period. This is the route the rubric assesses.

  1. Framing · about 5 minutes. Read the Fictional Context box on Student page 1 aloud, including the sentence that the Concord and the Zhel'ii belong to the wider story and appear later. Students should leave the framing knowing two things: the year 2387 is invented, and the handling of records is not.
  2. 1 · Build the Archive Vocabulary · about 8 minutes. Students place six terms from an exact-match bank. Do not pre-teach the definitions; the statements carry them. Circulate and check statement 3, which uses the term most students have never met.

    Answers, in statement order: provenance, archive, accession, source status, record, corroboration. The closing note under the task is the hinge into Task 5 — accession and corroboration are things an archive does; the other four are things a source has or needs. Say it out loud if students miss it.

  3. 2 · Trace a Record from Accession to Interpretation · about 10 minutes. Links 1 and 4 are given. Students complete link 2, what the archive adds, and link 3, what copying and moving can change, then name a link where a claim gains unearned weight.

    Most students name link 4, because interpretation is where a reader adds confidence. Accept link 2 or link 3 when the reasoning carries it: accession can make a record look authoritative simply by filing it, and transmission can strip the qualifiers that kept the original honest. The reasoning is assessed, not the link number.

  4. 3 · Sort the Orientation Sources by Status · about 12 minutes. The heaviest task. Students use the page 2 dossier to fill eight cells. Point them at the direction line: the three source kinds do not share a limit. If a student gives all four rows the same limit, the task has not been done — send them back to the source-kind column rather than supplying the answer.

Teaching procedure, continued

  1. 4 · Separate the Preserved Claim from Verified History · about 8 minutes. Students mark six items F or T, then write two short responses. The four F items are the Temporal Agricultural Archive, the Concord, the Zhel'ii, and resonance threading. The two T items are provenance and corroboration.

    Students who have not played may not recognise the Concord or the Zhel'ii. That is expected, and it is why the page 1 framing names them: they belong to the wider story and are not part of this orientation's evidence. In the written half, watch for the merge error — a student who decides the Archive's account is correct because the game let them finish the level has merged a story conclusion with a real-world claim. Redirect to the guiding question.

  2. 5 · Write the Archive Procedure · about 12 minutes. The culminating product. Students write an ordered procedure using at least four of the six terms and ending in a weight decision. Insist on the ending: a procedure that finishes with “so it is true” or “so it is false” has not done the work. The honest ending names what the record can be used to support, and what would have to be found before it could support more.
  3. 6 · Verify a Newly Discovered Record · about 5 minutes. The transfer check. The cellar record is deliberately outside the unit, so no dossier entry helps. Students who restate the orientation's own content instead of naming verification steps have recalled rather than transferred.
  4. Close · about 2 minutes. Ask one student to read the final step of their procedure aloud. Tell the class this is the procedure they will use on every case that follows, and that the first of those cases opens where the resonance map pointed.

Why this unit does not use Claim–Evidence–Reasoning

The culminating product here is a procedure, not an argument for a contested conclusion. There is no competing interpretation to defend against, so a Claim–Evidence–Reasoning frame would ask students to manufacture a dispute the evidence does not contain. Cases that culminate in a defended historical interpretation use the shared CER component; this orientation does not, and that is a deliberate design decision rather than an omission.

Route actually assessed

The route above is a single ordinary class period, and it is what the rubric measures. If your period is shorter, use the fallback priority on page 1 and record which criterion you dropped. Do not extend the unit across days to add optional exploration; the reasoning product is the assessment.

Formative checks

  • After Task 1, ask which two of the six terms name something the archive does. If the class cannot answer, Task 5 will not go well.
  • After Task 3, scan for four identical limits. That is the single most common failure in this unit.
  • After Task 4, ask for a claim the Archive preserves but has not verified. A student who cannot produce one has not yet separated preservation from proof.

Accessible adaptations actually used

The Accessible Mission is a four-page packet built alongside the Student Mission, not an enlarged copy of it. It preserves the same six task numbers, the same exact titles, the same dossier, and the same accepted answer space. The adaptations present are:

  • 1 · Build the Archive Vocabulary — the same six-term bank, unchanged, with the statements chunked one per line and larger placement fields.
  • 2 · Trace a Record from Accession to Interpretation — link 2 is completed as a worked model, so students write only link 3 and the unearned-weight explanation, with a sentence starter. The reasoning operation is preserved; the repetition is removed.
  • 3 · Sort the Orientation Sources by Status — the Nova row is completed as a model. Students complete the remaining three rows, which still requires producing all three distinct limits.
  • 4 · Separate the Preserved Claim from Verified History — the marking task is unchanged; the two written responses carry sentence starters.
  • 5 · Write the Archive Procedure — scaffolded as numbered steps with a starter for the closing weight decision. The four-term requirement is unchanged.
  • 6 · Verify a Newly Discovered Record — two separate labelled fields instead of one open field, so the second check is not lost.

Students using the Accessible packet must still produce three distinct limits in Task 3 and an ordered procedure ending in a weight decision in Task 5. Those two are the independent demonstration and are not scaffolded away. Bullets, labels, dictation, and scribed responses are acceptable throughout, and any prompt may be read aloud on request.

Likely misconceptions and redirects

What to listen for, and where to send it
MisconceptionRedirect
“It's in the archive, so it's true.”An archive guarantees custody, not accuracy. Ask what the archive actually did: it took the record in and wrote down where it came from. Neither step checks the claim.
“The scan is a measurement, so it beats the conversations.”An instrument reports one reading from one moment. That is a different limit, not a smaller one.
“The record is degrading, so something is attacking it.”The record itself says the cause is unknown. A record that states its own uncertainty should not be read past that point.
“Provenance means the record is old.”Provenance is where a record came from and whose hands it passed through. Age is not the same as a documented chain.

Source-status and fiction boundary

Everything in the dossier is invented. Do not let a correct in-world conclusion become a real-world claim: students may reasonably conclude that the orientation's records point to a river valley, and that conclusion says nothing whatever about real agricultural history. The first Core Case examines the real evidence separately and on its own terms.

If gameplay is unavailable

Nothing changes. The dossier on Student page 2 carries all four sources, and every task is answerable from it. No task requires a game state, a score, or a completed level, and none may be graded on any of them.

Completed exemplar. Both organizer links and the explanation are required.

Link 2 · Accession — the archive takes the record in, gives it an identifier, and writes down where it came from and who handed it over. It does not check whether the record is correct.

Link 3 · Transmission — as a record is copied, translated, summarised, or moved, details can be lost and the cautious wording of the original can drop away, so a hedged claim can end up sounding certain.

Where unearned weight enters — link 4, Interpretation. A reader treats the record as settled because it came out of an archive with a file number on it. The number records custody, not accuracy, so confidence appears that no one ever earned.

Acceptable alternatives. Link 2 or link 3 is fully creditable when the reasoning carries it — for example, that accession makes a record look authoritative simply by filing it, or that transmission strips the qualifiers that kept the original honest. Score the explanation, not the link number.

Completed exemplar for the culminating product. Ordered, five of the six terms used, ends in a weight decision.

  1. Accession. Take the record in, give it an identifier, and write down where it came from and who handed it over.
  2. Provenance. Trace its history back as far as the evidence goes: who made it, who held it, what happened to it in between. Write down where the trail stops, because that gap is part of the record's history too.
  3. Source status. Label what kind of source it is — someone's account, an official record, an instrument reading — and say what that kind can and cannot establish on its own.
  4. Corroboration. Look for a second, independent source reporting the same thing. Independent matters: two copies of one original are one source.
  5. Weight decision. Say what the record can now be used to support, and what would have to be found before it could support more.

Closing — the archive can now hold this record and say honestly what it is worth. It has not decided whether the record is true, and it does not need to. Keeping a record and vouching for it are two different jobs.

Acceptable variation. Step order may vary where the reasoning survives; provenance before accession is defensible. Four of the six terms is the requirement, and using five or six earns nothing extra. The one non-negotiable is the ending: a procedure that finishes with a verdict on truth rather than a decision about weight is not proficient, however fluent it is.

Completed exemplar. Two checks, each with a reason. The record is not one the unit supplied.

Check 1 — where it came from. Who wrote it, when, and how it reached the cellar. Without that trail there is no way to tell an eyewitness account from a much later copy, and a later copy can carry a date its author simply repeated from somewhere else.

Check 2 — whether anything independent agrees. Find a source that is not descended from this one and see whether it reports the same date. One record on its own gives one claim, not a confirmed date, and a village old enough to argue about deserves more than a single cellar document.

Acceptable variation. Any two genuine verification steps are creditable — physical examination of the document, the author's position and purpose, whether the date is stated or inferred, whether the record hedges. What is not creditable is restating the unit's own content, or answering with the Archive's conclusion instead of a check.
