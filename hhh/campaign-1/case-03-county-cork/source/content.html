Reconstruction and causation notice

The Archive puts you in one field, one cottage and one road, and gives the people there words you can follow. Those scenes are reconstructions. They were written for the game. No person in them is a real person and no ledger in them is a real ledger. This packet also contains a real letter written by a real man in December 1846. Telling those two kinds of source apart is part of the work.

This case asks two questions that are easy to run together. One is about a crop: what killed the potatoes? That question has a clear answer and you will reach it. The other is about people: why did about a million of them die? No single answer to that second question fits the evidence here — not the disease alone, and not any one decision alone. Keeping the two questions apart is what this case is for.

Case dossier · seven sources and the science behind them

Every source carries a STATUS line. The status is part of the source — read it every time. Sources A, B and C were written for the game. Source D was not.

A · The potato field STATUS: reconstructed · written for the game

“The crop stood green and sound a week ago; he woke to a stench and a field gone black overnight.”

Walking the rows, the Archivist finds the same plant end to end: “the same Lumper the poor have lived on for two generations, raised not from seed but from cuttings of cuttings.” Not a stalk stands clear of the rot. The soil probe reads the ground itself as sound — good tilth, no salt, no poison.

B · The cottage STATUS: reconstructed · written for the game

A woman at the hearth is asked what the family lives on. The potato, she says — “boiled in the morning, boiled at noon, boiled at night.” An acre of Lumpers fed the whole house from one harvest to the next.

“The oats go to the agent for the rent, every year, sold whether the belly's full or not, or the family's put out on the road. The pig's already gone the same way.”

C · The road, and the books STATUS: reconstructed · written for the game

A traveller walking west says the same black ruin stands in every field they have passed in four days. The verge is strewn with what people carried until they could not: a dropped pot, a child's shoe, the cold ash of roadside fires.

The estate ledger and the parish book are open together: the potato gone across the townland on one side; the same season's oats, barley, butter and live cattle logged, valued and consigned to the ports on the other.

D · A letter from West Cork STATUS: documented · a real letter, printed in a real newspaper

In December 1846 Nicholas Cummins, a magistrate of Cork, travelled to Skibbereen in the west of the county to see conditions for himself. He wrote to the Duke of Wellington and sent copies to the newspapers. The Times printed the letter on 24 December 1846. It opens:

“Being aware that I should have to witness scenes of frightful hunger, I provided myself with as much bread as five men could carry…”

What Cummins describes next is severe and is not reproduced here. He wrote it to persuade a government to act, and it did move public opinion in Britain.

Two kinds of thing. Cards A, B and C are a model of 1845 that somebody wrote. Card D has existed since 1846 and is in a newspaper archive today. Both are useful — not in the same way. Task 5 asks you to say how they differ.

E · What the poorest lived on STATUS: documented · historians writing from records

By the 1830s about a third of the population got about ninety per cent of what it ate from the potato — most heavily in the south and west. A labouring man in the west ate ten to fourteen pounds of potatoes a day.

One acre of ridged beds could yield nearly six tonnes — a year's food for a family off a plot too small to feed them any other way. County Cork alone had a quarter of a million acres under potatoes.

Other food was grown in Ireland all through these years. This card is about what the poorest ate, not about everything the island produced.

F · How they held the ground STATUS: documented · historians writing from records

The cottier class numbered more than three million people in the early 1840s. A cottier held a cabin and a potato plot and paid for them mostly in labour — commonly about two hundred days a year — under an unwritten agreement that could be ended at a moment's notice.

Cottiers rarely handled cash and were persistently in debt. In 1841 nearly half of rural families lived in single-room mud-walled cabins.

A household with no money cannot buy food, whatever is for sale.

Background · outside the Archive's scenes STATUS: documented · modern plant science

The organism is Phytophthora infestans. It is an oomycete — a water mould. It is not a true fungus, although it looks like one and was called one for a long time.

Spores land on a wet leaf and the organism grows into the living tissue. Dark blotches spread through the leaf. In damp air a fine white growth fruits on the underside of the leaf at the edge of the blotch and releases more spores to the wind and the rain. Spores washed down into the soil rot the potatoes underground. It thrives in cool, damp weather and stalls in hot dry weather. One blotch can make thousands of spores in under five days.

This is how the disease behaves anywhere, not part of the Archive's scenes. You will need it from Task 4 onward.

STATUS: debated · two historians read the same records differently G · The food that left. Food kept leaving Ireland while people starved. Almost four thousand vessels carried food from Ireland to British ports in 1847, and more than three million live animals were exported between 1846 and 1850, along with grain, butter, fish and vegetables.

Austin Bourke (1976) counted the government's grain returns and found that by 1847 grain imports to Ireland were larger than grain exports. Christine Kinealy argues those returns are flawed, under-record what left, cover only grain rather than the whole food trade, and cannot measure what was lost in food terms — and that imports only became significant after the spring of 1847, leaving a gap over the winter of 1846–47.

Nothing in this packet weighs the food that left against the food that was needed. That is why Task 7 gives you a mark for a claim this evidence cannot decide.

Teacher Guide

Launch sheet

This case runs over three ordinary class periods. Students arrive already holding an explanation — “the potato blight caused the Irish famine” — and the packet is built so that they revise it themselves against a real historical control rather than being told it is wrong. The subject matter is heavy. Read the note on emotional load on page 4 before you teach it.

Before class

Print the eight-page Student Mission or the Accessible Mission. Both carry the whole dossier and every figure, so either packet is completely teachable with no device in the room. Decide before period 1 which route you are running, and read the reconstruction and causation notice on learner page 1 yourself first.

Central case resolution

Late blight, an oomycete, destroyed the potato crop; that part is settled science and students should reach it confidently. The death toll is a different question. It followed from the crop failure meeting deep dependence among the poorest, land and labour arrangements that left them with no money and no security, unequal access to the food that remained, and relief that arrived late, changed and was withdrawn — repeated across four seasons. No single one of those, biological or political, is the answer.

Essential evidence

One comparison carries this case. In 1845 Belgium lost almost 90% of its potato harvest and Ireland lost about 30% — and Belgian famine mortality ran 30–40% above normal while Ireland's ran at about three times normal. Same organism, same years, larger crop loss, far smaller death toll. That is the evidence that refutes “blight alone” without any assertion from you, and it is the spine of 4 · Trace the Blight, Then Test It. If one thing survives the three periods, it should be that the size of a crop failure does not set the size of a famine.

Likely sticking point

Two, pulling opposite ways. Most students stop at the disease. A smaller group, once they meet the export evidence, swings to a single political cause instead — which is the same error with a different villain. 7 · Weigh Five Claims is built to catch both: Claim 2 is the biological single cause and Claim 4 is the political one, and Claim 4 is undecidable here rather than false.

What to collect

Collect the whole packet at the end of period 3. 8 · Explain How a Crop Failure Became a Famine is the graded culminating product; 9 · Tell a Crop Failure from a Famine is the transfer check and uses an invented district no dossier card answers.

Teacher framing line

“You already know the answer to half of this. A disease killed the potatoes — you will be able to explain exactly how by tomorrow. But the same disease killed the potatoes in Belgium that year, and killed a bigger share of them, and Belgium did not lose a million people. So the disease is the answer to one question and not to the other. The second question is the one historians are still working on, and by Friday you will be able to work on it too.”

Classroom and technical fallback

If devices fail mid-period, switch to the no-game route and change nothing else. If you lose a period, protect 4 · Trace the Blight, Then Test It, 6 · Work the Causes Map and 8 · Explain How a Crop Failure Became a Famine; 1 · Build the Case Vocabulary and 3 · Put the Failures in Order can be finished as homework. Dropping 5 · Decide What Each Source Can Show removes the source-status evidence from the rubric — that is a modified assessment route, and the source and limitation criterion can no longer be scored.

Success criteria

Students can say what late blight does to a potato plant; can explain why Belgium's larger crop loss and smaller death toll matters; can name two conditions with evidence; can describe two conditions acting on each other rather than listing them; and can finish a paragraph about the famine without blaming any single cause.

Complete teaching procedure / task route — period 1

Three ordinary class periods. This is the route the rubric assesses.

  1. Framing · about 4 minutes. Read the reconstruction and causation notice on Student page 1 aloud, both paragraphs. The second is the one that matters: this case asks a question about a crop and a question about people, and the answer to the first is not the answer to the second. Do not preview the Belgium comparison. Students should meet it as evidence, not as a headline.
  2. 1 · Build the Case Vocabulary · about 8 minutes. Six terms from an exact-match bank. Do not pre-teach the definitions; the statements carry them.

    Answers, in statement order: blight, clone, cottier, export, famine, relief. The closing note under the task is the hinge of the whole case — a blight kills a crop, a famine kills people — and it is worth saying aloud.

  3. Read the dossier · about 12 minutes. Student page 2. Seven sources with STATUS lines. Ask the class to read each status line aloud before the card. Point at cards A–C and card D together and ask what kind of thing each one is; that question is the whole of Task 5 row one against row two. On the game route, play Level 3 here instead and then read page 2 quickly; it carries the same evidence, and more than the level has.
  4. 2 · Record a First Explanation · about 7 minutes. Students write the explanation they already hold and name one check. Its directions restrict them to the three reconstructed scenes, so it records a starting point rather than a conclusion. Most classes write some version of “the disease killed the food.” Do not correct it. Tell them they will come back to this page, and mean it — the last three minutes of period 3 are for exactly that.
  5. 3 · Put the Failures in Order · about 15 minutes. Part A is 1846, the year that lost over three quarters of the crop. Part B is County Cork, read off the place figure. Part C is the assessed reasoning: a household that eats what it grows has one harvest of margin, so the first failure empties the store and the second takes the seed, the rent and any chance of recovery with it.

    The timeline carries two strands on purpose. Ask the class where the relief entries sit relative to the crop entries. The largest measures arrive in 1847 — after the second and worst failure — and the soup kitchens close the same summer they reach three million people. Students do not need to judge that yet; they need to notice it, because Claim 5 in Task 7 depends on the sequence being visible.

  6. Close period 1 · about 3 minutes. Ask one student to read their Task 2 first explanation aloud without comment. Tell the class that tomorrow they find out whether it is right.
Why Task 2 is not corrected. The single-cause explanation is not a misconception to be pre-empted; it is the case's own starting position and the thing the evidence has to move. A class told on day one that the answer is complicated learns a slogan. A class that writes down “the blight did it” and then meets Belgium changes its mind, which is the actual historical skill.
Emotional load. This case involves the deaths of about a million people and the eviction of hundreds of thousands more. The packet is deliberately restrained: Source D quotes only the opening line of the Cummins letter and says in print that what follows is severe and is not reproduced. Students with family connections to this history may find the material personal in a way the rest of the campaign is not. Say at the start that the deaths in this case were real, that the packet treats them as evidence because that is what the archive does, and that anyone who needs to step out may.

Formative checks

  • After Task 1, ask the difference between a blight and a famine. If the class cannot answer, Task 4 Part B will not go well.
  • After Task 4 Part B, scan for any answer that makes the Irish blight worse or different from the Belgian one. That is the single most common wrong reading of the panel.
  • After Task 5, scan for five identical limits, and for rows one and two sharing a status. Those are the two failures this table exists to catch.
  • After Task 7, scan the Claim 4 column. A class with no question marks anywhere has swapped one single cause for another.

Teaching procedure — period 2

  1. 4 · Trace the Blight, Then Test It · about 22 minutes. The heaviest single task and the spine of the case. Stages 1 and 5 are given; students write 2, 3 and 4 from the Background block, then answer Part B from the comparison.

    Expected stages: 2 the organism grows into the living leaf tissue and dark blotches spread through it; 3 in damp air a fine white growth fruits on the underside of the leaf and releases thousands more spores to wind and rain; 4 spores washed into the soil rot the potatoes underground, so the part people eat is destroyed too.

    Part B is the assessed reasoning and the moment the case turns. The answer is not that the blight was worse in Ireland — the figures say the opposite for 1845. It is that the same organism destroying a larger share of a national crop produced a far smaller share of deaths, so the organism cannot be what set the death toll; something about Ireland, not about the disease, did. Watch for “Belgium had better weather” or “Belgium's blight was weaker”: send those students back to the bars, which hold the disease constant and the loss higher in Belgium.

  2. 5 · Decide What Each Source Can Show · about 22 minutes. Five statuses from an exact-match bank and then ten written cells — eight on the Accessible edition, where row one is modelled. The direction line is the one to point at: the five rows do not share a limit.

    Statuses, in row order: reconstructed, documented, debated, inferred, modeled. Rows one and two are the pair worth the time. Both describe hunger in 1840s Ireland; one was written for a game in our century and one was written by a magistrate in Skibbereen in December 1846 and printed in a newspaper. If a student gives them the same status, the task has not been done.

  3. Close period 2 · about 3 minutes. Ask what Belgium proves. Accept only an answer about the disease not being enough, not an answer about Belgium being luckier.

Teaching procedure — period 3

  1. 6 · Work the Causes Map · about 15 minutes. The map is given; drawing it is not the learning target, and using it is. Part A asks for two condition letters and then an interaction. The commonest incomplete answer describes A and then describes B. Push with one question: “what did the first one do to the second one?” Strong pairs include A with C — the more of a household's food came from one crop, the more the food it did not own mattered when that crop went — and B with D, since a cottier holding more than a quarter of an acre had to give up the ground that fed him to qualify for relief.

    Part B answers from Task 4: an account keeping only the trigger box cannot explain why Belgium, with a larger crop loss, did not lose a comparable share of its people.

  2. 7 · Weigh Five Claims · about 12 minutes. Marks: Claim 1 Y · Claim 2 N · Claim 3 Y · Claim 4 ? · Claim 5 N.

    Claim 4 is the one to spend time on. It is ? rather than N because the packet carries both published positions and the reason they cannot be reconciled from these records: Bourke counted the official grain returns and found imports exceeding exports by 1847; Kinealy holds that those returns under-record what left, cover only grain, and cannot measure food loss, and that imports only mattered after spring 1847. A student who marks Claim 4 N has decided a live scholarly dispute on evidence that does not reach it — and a student who marks it Y has done the same in the other direction.

  3. 8 · Explain How a Crop Failure Became a Famine · about 18 minutes. The culminating product. Four required components are printed on the Student page. Insist on the third: an explanation that lists causes in a row has not met the objective, however many it lists. Multiple causation means causes acting on each other.

    The two responses this task exists to reject sit on opposite sides. “The blight caused the famine” under-explains. “The British government caused the famine” replaces one sufficient cause with another and is not what the packet supports either. Evidence and reasoning architecture

The case runs one operation — establish a cause, then test whether it is sufficient — and rebuilds the explanation from what survives. The path is: a disease is named (mechanism) → it took more of Belgium's crop and far fewer of Belgium's people (control) → so it explains the crop and not the deaths (insufficiency) → what stood between them? (conditions) → four conditions, each evidenced, none ranked (map) → acting on one another rather than in a queue (interaction) → across four failed seasons (time).

The five claims in Task 7 are not decoration. Claim 2 is the single biological cause the audit required qualifying; Claim 5 is the single political cause students reach for once they meet the export and relief evidence; Claim 4 is undecidable here, the harder and more honest mark. A class that converts this case into “it was really the British government” has learned the lesson backwards.

Instructional emphasis

Spend the time on Part B of Task 4 and component three of Task 8. A student who completes every organizer and still lists four causes in a row has not met the objective; a student whose prose is thin but who explains how two conditions acted on each other, and states a limit, has.

Background · outside the Archive's scenes STATUS: documented · modern plant science

The organism is Phytophthora infestans. It is an oomycete — a water mould. It is not a true fungus, though it looks like one.

What it does, in order:

  • A spore lands on a wet leaf.
  • The organism grows into the living leaf. Dark blotches spread.
  • In damp air a fine white growth appears on the underside of the leaf and releases more spores to wind and rain.
  • Spores wash into the soil and rot the potatoes underground.

It likes cool, damp weather. It stalls in hot dry weather. One blotch can make thousands of spores in under five days.

Case dossier · part 3 · conditions in Ireland

E · What the poorest lived on STATUS: documented · historians writing from records

By the 1830s, about a third of the population got about ninety per cent of its food from the potato. This was heaviest in the south and west.

A labouring man in the west ate ten to fourteen pounds of potatoes a day.

One acre of ridged beds could grow nearly six tonnes — a year's food for a family, off a plot too small to feed them any other way. County Cork alone had a quarter of a million acres under potatoes.

Other food was grown in Ireland all through these years. This card is about what the poorest ate, not about everything the island produced.

F · How they held the ground STATUS: documented · historians writing from records

The cottier class was more than three million people in the early 1840s.

A cottier held a cabin and a potato plot. He paid for them mostly in work — usually about two hundred days a year. The agreement was not written down and could be ended at a moment's notice.

Cottiers rarely handled money and were nearly always in debt. In 1841, nearly half of country families lived in one-room mud-walled cabins.

A household with no money cannot buy food, however much food is for sale.

STATUS: debated · two historians read the same records differently G · The food that left. Food kept leaving Ireland while people starved. In 1847, almost four thousand ships carried food from Ireland to British ports. Between 1846 and 1850, more than three million live animals were sent out, plus grain, butter, fish and vegetables.

Austin Bourke counted the government's grain records. He found that by 1847, more grain was coming in than going out.

Christine Kinealy says those records are unreliable. They under-count what left, they only cover grain, and they cannot measure how much food was actually lost. She also says food only started arriving in real amounts after spring 1847 — leaving a gap over the winter before.
