Guiding question

Watering a field is supposed to help it. How can watering ruin the soil instead — and how much of a region's history can that one fact explain?

Reconstruction and scale notice

The Archive puts you in one field, one river bank and one records room, and gives the people there words you can follow. Those scenes are reconstructions. They were written for the game. No person in them is a real person, and no tablet in them is a real tablet.

This case also asks two questions of very different sizes. The small one is about one field: can watering it turn the soil salty enough to hurt the crop? The big one is about a whole region across centuries: how much did salty soil have to do with the decline of southern Mesopotamia? The evidence in this packet answers the small question well. It does not settle the big one, and historians still argue about it. Holding the two apart is what this case is for.

Case dossier

Four sources. Each one carries a STATUS line. The status is part of the source — read it every time.

A · At the fields STATUS: reconstructed

“The overseer floods the fields from the channels again and again, and crops the land every season without the old fallow rest.”

“Every channel feeds water onto the fields and not one drains it off again.”

Walking the plots, the Archivist finds a pattern: “By the channel-heads, where the water runs fast and sinks and is gone, the soil is nearly clean and the grain still stands. Out at the far flats, where the water pools and lies and waits for the sun to take it, the crust is thickest and the crop is dead.” The device reads the pale crust as salt.

B · At the river and the marsh STATUS: reconstructed

“Where the river and the marsh keep the water moving, the ground stays sweet as the current carries the salt away.”

The device samples the river: only a faint trace of salt, and the flow never stops. It is the same water the channels carry inland. The reeds on the bank stand thick and green in dark, almost salt-free soil.

C · In the scribes' room STATUS: reconstructed

The Archive reads the temple tallies: harvests falling year on year, and baskets of wheat giving way to barley season on season. Beside each falling yield the scribes set down a reason — the gods' favour withdrawn, the offerings too small — and prescribe more grain to the temple and more prayer.

Nowhere in the clay: salt, standing water, or a field that will not drain.

D · What scholars have made of the real records STATUS: debated · a reading of real documents, and an argument about it

Real administrative tablets from the southern plain do survive. In 1958 two researchers read them as showing wheat's share of the crop falling over centuries while barley's rose, and argued that salt building up in the soil was the reason.

In 1985 another researcher challenged that reading. He disputed the translation of key parts and argued farmers may have moved to barley because it also gave more grain and coped better with an uneven season. The argument is not settled.

Background · outside the Archive's scenes STATUS: documented · modern soil and water science

All river water carries a little dissolved salt. When irrigation water soaks into a field, the water can go down and away only if there is somewhere for it to drain to. Where there is not, the water table rises. Once the water table sits within a few metres of the surface, water creeps back up toward the surface, and the sun and the heat pull it off as vapour. The water leaves. The salt it carried does not. Salt keeps arriving and stops leaving, so it collects where the roots are. Only water moving downward and then draining away carries salt out of a field.

This is how irrigated soil behaves anywhere, not part of the Archive's scenes. You will need it from Task 4 onward.

Use Source A only. Do not use the Background block or Sources B, C or D yet.

In your own words, what pattern does the walk across the plots show?
Name one thing you would have to check before calling that pattern the cause — and say why.

Teacher Guide

Launch sheet

This case runs over two ordinary class periods. It has a real physical mechanism at its centre and a real argument at its edge, and the whole design exists to keep those two apart. Students should leave able to explain confidently how one field salts, and able to say plainly that the same evidence does not explain the fall of a civilisation.

Before class

Print the eight-page Student Mission or the eleven-page Accessible Mission. Both carry the full dossier — Student page 2, Accessible pages 2–3 — and every figure, so either packet is completely teachable with no device in the room. Decide before period 1 which route you are running.

Central case resolution

Irrigating a nearly level plain with a shallow water table and no way to drain raises that water table; water then leaves upward as vapour and the dissolved salt it carried stays in the soil. Salt arrives and does not leave, so it builds up in the root zone and yields fall. The marsh disproves any explanation blaming the water itself: the same river water there keeps moving, and the salt goes with it.

The two routes

Either route assesses the same nine tasks against the same rubric
RouteWhat students doWhat you must still supply

Essential evidence

Four kinds of evidence with four different statuses: the Archive's reconstructed scenes, modern soil and water science, a contested scholarly reading of real records, and an inference drawn from modern measurements. Those four must not collapse into one in 6 · Decide What Each Kind of Evidence Can Show. The paired cross-sections on Student page 4 are the spine of the case: both sides get the same water, and only one thing differs. If one thing survives the two periods, it should be that what makes a field salt is not bad water but water that cannot leave.

Likely sticking point

Two, and they pull in opposite directions. Some students conclude the farmers were simply careless — send them to Student page 3: rainfall there is under 100 mm a year, so irrigation was not optional. Others slide from “the field salted” to “that is why Sumer fell” in one step. That slide is exactly what 7 · Weigh Five Claims exists to stop.

What to collect

Collect the whole packet at the end of period 2. 8 · Write What the Evidence Will Carry is the graded culminating product; 9 · Test the Reasoning on a New District is the transfer check and uses an invented district the case never supplied.

Teacher framing line

“Everything these farmers did was sensible. The rain would not grow a crop, so they brought the river to the field. What none of them could see is that every year the water arrived carrying a little salt, and every year the sun took the water back and left the salt behind. By the end of this case you will be able to explain that exactly — and you will also be able to say what it does not explain.”

Classroom and technical fallback

If devices fail mid-period, switch to the no-game route and change nothing else. If you lose a period, protect 4 · Trace the Water and the Salt, 7 · Weigh Five Claims and 8 · Write What the Evidence Will Carry; 1 · Build the Salinization Vocabulary and 3 · Place the Fields in Their Setting can be finished as homework. Dropping 6 · Decide What Each Kind of Evidence Can Show removes the source-status evidence from the rubric — that is a modified assessment route, and the evidence and qualification criterion can no longer be scored.

Lesson overview

Two class periods. Students learn six terms, read three reconstructed scenes and one contested scholarly reading, orient to a plain where rain cannot grow a crop, trace how irrigation water leaves a field while its salt does not, test what a change from wheat to barley proves, sort four kinds of evidence, judge five claims of deliberately different sizes, and write a causal explanation that states its own limit. The case is about a real mechanism and about the discipline of not over-reading it.

Guiding historical question

Watering a field is supposed to help it. How can watering ruin the soil instead — and how much of a region's history can that one fact explain?

Measurable objectives

  1. Place all six case terms correctly from an exact-match bank.
  2. Read a pattern off a field survey, name one check it needs, and explain from the setting why irrigation was necessary and drainage hard.
  3. Complete the salt path and explain the field-and-marsh difference as departure of water, not quality of water.
  4. State why a crop change consistent with salinity does not prove it, and supply a rival explanation.
  5. Give four kinds of evidence four different statuses and four appropriate limits.
  6. Mark five claims across two scales, including one the case cannot decide, and name evidence that would move it.
  7. Write a causal explanation with mechanism, two sourced pieces of evidence, a stated limit, and a claim at the right strength.
  8. Apply the mechanism to an unfamiliar district and propose a first change to test.

Success criteria

Students can say what happens to salt when water leaves as vapour; can explain why the marsh does not salt on the same water; can name something the crop record cannot prove; and can finish a paragraph about Sumer without either blaming everything on salt or dismissing it.

Complete teaching procedure / task route — period 1

Two ordinary class periods. This is the route the rubric assesses.

  1. Framing · about 5 minutes. Read the Reconstruction and scale notice on Student page 1 aloud, both paragraphs. The second paragraph is the one that matters: this case asks a small question about one field and a big question about a whole region, and only the small one gets settled here. Say it in your own words as well.
  2. 1 · Build the Salinization Vocabulary · about 10 minutes. Six terms from an exact-match bank. Do not pre-teach the definitions; the statements carry them.

    Answers, in statement order: alluvial plain, drainage, evaporation, fallow, water table, salinization. The closing note under the task is the hinge of the whole case — drainage takes the salt with the water, evaporation leaves the salt behind. Say it out loud if students skim past it.

  3. Read the dossier · about 10 minutes. Student page 2, Accessible pages 2–3. Four sources, four STATUS lines, plus the documented Background block. Ask the class to read each status line aloud before the card. On the game route, play Level 2 here instead and then read the dossier quickly; the two carry the same evidence.
  4. 2 · Read the Field Before You Explain It · about 8 minutes. Students state the pattern from the field walk and name one check. Deliberately placed before the Background block is needed, so it records a starting point rather than a conclusion. The pattern is that the crust is worst where water stands longest and nearly absent where water runs through. Accept any genuine outside check — testing the soil, comparing with ground that is watered differently, looking at more than one field. Do not correct a first reading for being incomplete; that is what it is for.
  5. 3 · Place the Fields in Their Setting · about 15 minutes. Part A is the Gulf, the place the diagram marks at the end of the plain as the one outlet. Part B wants rainfall under 100 mm a year, which will not grow a grain crop, so the river had to be led to the field. Part C wants the consequence of a nearly level plain: there is no slope to carry water off again, so water that arrives tends to stay.

    This task exists to stop the reading that the farmers were careless. Irrigation there was not a choice. Point at the rainfall figure rather than saying so.

  6. 4 · Trace the Water and the Salt · about 20 minutes. The spine of the case and the heaviest single task. Stages 1 and 5 are given; students write stages 2, 3 and 4, then explain the field-and-marsh difference.

    Expected stages: 2 the water soaks in but cannot drain away, so the water table under the field rises toward the surface; 3 near the surface the sun and heat evaporate the water and it leaves as vapour; 4 the salt cannot evaporate, so it stays in the soil where the roots are.

    Part B is the assessed reasoning. The answer is not that the river water is cleaner than the field water — the packet says twice that it is the same water. The difference is that in the marsh the water keeps leaving, and carries its salt away with it, while on the field the only way out is upward, and salt cannot go that way. Watch for “the marsh soil is just better”: send those students back to the shared line above the two cross-sections.

  7. Close period 1 · about 2 minutes. Ask one student to say what happens to salt when water evaporates. Tell the class that tomorrow they find out how much that explains.
The comparison is the proof. The two cross-sections are drawn from the same water under the same sun and differ in exactly one thing. That is what makes Task 4 an argument rather than an illustration, and it is why the marsh belongs in the packet at all.

Teaching procedure — period 2

  1. 5 · Test What the Crop Change Proves · about 15 minutes. Part A is barley. Part B is the assessed move: the measurements and the record agree, and agreement is not proof, because other things could produce the same record. Part C wants a rival explanation — barley also gave more grain, or coped better with an uneven season — which is the objection published against the salinization reading in 1985.

    The commonest wrong answer to Part B is a restatement: “because it doesn't prove it.” Push for the reason. Ask what else would make a farmer plant more barley, and the answer arrives on its own.

  2. 6 · Decide What Each Kind of Evidence Can Show · about 18 minutes. Students place four statuses from an exact-match bank and then write eight cells — six on the Accessible edition, where the first row is modelled. The direction line is the one to point at: the four rows do not share a limit.

    Statuses, in row order: reconstructed, documented, debated, inferred. The fourth row is the hard one and it is the one worth the time: the measurements themselves are documented, but using them to judge an ancient field is an inference, because nobody measured that field. If a student gives all four rows the same limit, the task has not been done — send them back to the STATUS lines rather than supplying the answer.

Two rows may legitimately share a phrase in the can show column. The four limits may not collapse, because the four statuses are what the task assesses.

Why row four is not row two. Students often merge them because both involve modern science. The distinction is the one the case is built on: a number can be exact and still not answer a question about a place where it was never taken. That is the same move Task 5 asks for, applied to a source rather than to a record.

Why this case does not use Claim–Evidence–Reasoning

The culminating product is an explanation of a physical process plus an explicit statement of how far it reaches. A Claim–Evidence–Reasoning frame would ask students to defend one position in the region-scale dispute, and the honest answer to that dispute is that the evidence in front of them does not decide it. A frame built for taking a side would therefore push them toward exactly the overclaim this case exists to prevent. Cases that culminate in a defended interpretation use the shared CER component; this one does not, and that is a deliberate design decision rather than an omission.

  1. 7 · Weigh Five Claims · about 15 minutes. Marks: Claim 1 Y · Claim 2 N · Claim 3 N · Claim 4 Y · Claim 5 ?.

    The three-mark scheme is the point. Claim 5 is ? rather than N because this packet contains nothing that weighs it either way — that is a different situation from Claim 3, where the words “one and only” are contradicted by evidence the packet does supply. Students who mark everything N have flattened the distinction the task assesses.

    Claim 2 is worth a minute of discussion. The scribes did record the gods as the cause, and that record is real evidence — of what people wrote down. It is not evidence of what was happening to the soil. A recorded explanation and a working explanation are different things, and students meet that distinction again in every later case.

  2. 8 · Write What the Evidence Will Carry · about 20 minutes. The culminating product. Four required components are printed on the Student page. Insist on all four, and especially the fourth: an explanation that ends by blaming the fall of Sumer on salt has not met the learning goal, however fluent the mechanism paragraph above it was.

    The two responses this task exists to reject sit on opposite sides. “Salt destroyed Sumer” overstates. “We can't really know anything about it” dismisses. The proficient answer holds both: the field mechanism is well supported, and how much it contributed to the region's wider decline is argued.

  3. 9 · Test the Reasoning on a New District · about 8 minutes. The transfer check. The district is invented and the packet says so, so no dossier card helps. Matching parts: flat land, long irrigation, a risen water table, salt worst farthest from the drain, and a switch to a tougher crop. What it cannot establish: it is a snapshot with no before-and-after, and the crop switch could again have other reasons. A first change to test is drainage — more ditches, or ditches reaching the far fields — because the whole mechanism depends on whether water can leave.

  4. Close · about 3 minutes. Ask one student to read their fourth sentence from Task 8 aloud. Tell the class the next case asks what happens when a crop fails for a reason that has nothing to do with the soil.

The two periods above are the route the rubric measures: period 1 ends after Task 4, period 2 covers Tasks 5 to 9. If you have only one period, use the fallback priority on page 1 and record which criterion you dropped. Optional game exploration is never part of the assessed route and is never scored.

Formative checks

  • After Task 1, ask what evaporation leaves behind. If the class cannot answer, Task 4 will not go well.
  • After Task 4 Part B, scan for any answer that makes the marsh water different from the field water. That is the single most common wrong mechanism.
  • After Task 6, scan for four identical limits. That is the most common failure in a source-status table.
  • After Task 7, scan the Claim 5 column. A class with no question marks anywhere has not met the case's central distinction.

Accessible adaptations actually used

The Accessible Mission is an eleven-page packet built alongside the Student Mission, not an enlarged copy. Same nine task numbers, exact titles and accepted answer space; the same dossier split across two pages so no page mixes reading load with writing load. The adaptations are:

  • 1 · Build the Salinization Vocabulary — the same six-term bank, unchanged, each statement chunked onto its own line with a larger field beneath it.
  • 2 · Read the Field Before You Explain It — two labelled fields with sentence starters, so the second half is not lost.
  • 3 · Place the Fields in Their Setting — the same side-view diagram at a larger size and the same modern report, with the report reduced to four labelled facts.
  • 4 · Trace the Water and the Salt — stage 2 is worked as a model alongside the fixed first and last stages. Students write stages 3 and 4 and the full Part B explanation, with a starter for Part B only.
  • 5 · Test What the Crop Change Proves — the same bars and the same three parts, with two candidate rival explanations offered in Part C so the learner justifies a choice rather than generating one from nothing.
  • 6 · Decide What Each Kind of Evidence Can Show — the first row is modelled. Students complete three rows, still producing three statuses and three distinct limits.
  • 7 · Weigh Five Claims — marking unchanged, all five claims kept; Part B names which claim to work on so the choice does not consume the reasoning.
  • 8 · Write What the Evidence Will Carry — the same four required components, split into four labelled fields with starters instead of one open box. The requirement is unchanged; the organisation is supplied.
  • 9 · Test the Reasoning on a New District — the report restated as a bulleted list, and two separate labelled fields instead of one.

Accessible students must still produce three distinct limits in Task 6, the full Part B mechanism explanation in Task 4, all five marks in Task 7, and all four components in Task 8. Those are the independent demonstration and are not scaffolded away. Bullets, dictation and scribed responses are acceptable throughout.

Evidence and reasoning architecture

The case runs one reasoning operation — build a physical cause, then bound it — across two scales. The path is: watering is necessary here (setting) → water arrives and cannot leave (scenes) → salt accumulates (mechanism) → the marsh proves it is departure, not water quality, that matters (comparison) → the crop record agrees (measurements plus reading) → but agreement is not proof and the reading is disputed (limit) → so the field claim stands and the region claim does not follow. Every task after the vocabulary sits on that path.

The five claims in Task 7 are not decoration. Claim 3 is the overclaim the game's own closing account invites, Claim 5 is the forward-looking statement the audit flagged, and Claim 2 is the explanation the people in the case actually recorded. Marking Claim 5 undecidable rather than false is the harder move and the one worth the discussion time.

The pattern — the salt crust is not spread evenly. It is thinnest, or gone, at the heads of the channels where the water runs fast and sinks away, and the grain there is still standing. It is thickest out on the far flats where the water pools and sits until the sun takes it, and there the crop is dead. Completed exemplar. Three stages and Part B are all required.

Stage 2 · The water soaks in, and there is no drain — the water sinks into the soil, but with no channel to carry it off again it has nowhere to go. It adds to the water already under the field, so the water table rises closer to the surface each time the field is watered.

Stage 3 · The water reaches the surface again — once the water table is close under the surface, water creeps back up toward it, and the sun and the heat turn that water into vapour and take it away.

Stage 4 · The water is gone. What about the salt? — the salt cannot turn into vapour, so it does not go with the water. It is left behind in the soil, near the surface and around the roots, and a little more is added every time the field is watered.

Part B · what actually makes the difference — it is not the water. The packet says twice that the marsh and the fields get the same river water, and the device finds only a faint trace of salt in it. The difference is whether the water leaves. In the marsh the water keeps moving through and out, and it carries its dissolved salt away with it, so nothing accumulates. On the field the only way out is upward as vapour, and salt cannot travel that way, so the water leaves and the salt stays. Salt arrives on both, but only one of them exports it.

Acceptable variation. Wording is open and bullets are acceptable. Proficient work must contain two things: that the water table rises because the water cannot drain, and that evaporation removes the water but not the salt. Part B must name departure of water rather than quality of water. Answers saying the river water is cleaner, or that marsh soil is naturally better, have the mechanism wrong even if the three stages are filled in correctly, because the figure and both learner pages hold the water constant on purpose.
