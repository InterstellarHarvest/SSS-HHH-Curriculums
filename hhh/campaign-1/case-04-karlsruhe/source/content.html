Reconstruction and attribution notice

The Archive puts you in a laboratory in 1909 and gives the chemist there words you can follow. Those scenes are reconstructions. They were written for the game. The man in them is based on a real person, but nothing he says is a real thing anybody said. This packet also contains a real document — a patent filed in 1909 and still in the public record. This case asks two questions that are easy to run together. One is about chemistry: why does the reaction give so little, and what can be done about it? The other is about people: whose work turned a result on a bench into something that grows food? The answer to the second question is not one name. Keeping the two questions apart is what this case is for.

Case dossier · part 1 · the bench, the patent, the historian

Every source carries a STATUS line. The status is part of the source — read it every time. Source A was written for the game. Source B was filed in 1909 and still exists.

A · The Archive's laboratory STATUS: reconstructed · written for the game

On the bench: a stout steel tube wrapped in heating coils, fed by two gas lines, watched by a pressure gauge. Nitrogen and hydrogen go in. Almost nothing comes out. The chemist calls it a failure.

The Archive's instruments read the vessel and disagree. The seals hold. The pressure is steady. Nothing is leaking. The gas leaving the tube is almost all unreacted nitrogen and hydrogen with only a thread of ammonia — because the reaction runs forward and back at once and settles at a balance. It has not failed. It has come to rest.

The chemist, at a chalkboard: cold, the gases will make a handsome amount of ammonia and take a hundred years about it; hot, they hurry and give him nothing. He has been laying out trays of metals in the path of the gas to see which wakes the reaction up. Osmium. Uranium. Why those and not iron, he cannot yet say.

The notebooks show a few parts in a hundred, run after run. A later margin holds the turn: do not throw the spent gas away.

B · A patent filed in 1909 STATUS: documented · a real document, still in the public record

United States Patent 1,202,995, Production of ammonia. Filed 13 August 1909. Granted 31 October 1916. Assigned to the German chemical company BASF.

It names two inventors, both living at Karlsruhe: Fritz Haber and Robert Le Rossignol.

What the document itself states:

  • the process must be run continuously, under pressure, and can be operated at 150 atmospheres;
  • higher pressure increases the amount of ammonia in the gas;
  • the catalyst is worked at between 500 and 1000 °C;
  • gases that have had their ammonia removed are passed over the catalyst again.

A patent records what its inventors claimed and wanted to protect. It is not a measurement of a factory, and it says nothing about what happened on any particular day.

C · A historian on who did what STATUS: documented · a historian writing from records

Anthony S. Travis, in a lecture given to the Royal Society of Chemistry and published in 2015, sets out the people and the order:

  • Haber calculated that about 8% ammonia could be reached at about 600 °C and 200 atmospheres.
  • Robert Le Rossignol, an English assistant, built the steel apparatus and the compressor, and invented the valve that held the pressure. He designed the complete apparatus.
  • Manganese, iron, chromium and nickel gave little. Osmium worked — but only about 100 grams could be bought, probably most of the world's supply.
  • Alwin Mittasch at BASF screened catalyst after catalyst until promoted iron proved right, in 1910.
  • Carl Bosch and engineer Franz Lappe found hydrogen was getting into the steel and making it brittle. In February 1911 they answered it with a double-walled converter, vented through small holes.
  • Production at Oppau began on 9 September 1913, at about twenty tonnes a day, soon about thirty.

On the date of the demonstration that convinced BASF, this historian is careful. He gives 1 July 1909 as one version of events — and says the first run failed when a seal sprang, so Bosch left, and the ammonia only appeared the next day.

Case dossier · part 2 · the process, the chemistry, the count

D · The industry's own account STATUS: documented · a technical review written for the fertilizer industry

Max Appl, writing for the International Fertilizer Industry Association in 1997:

  • The reaction gives off heat and takes up less room than the gases it is made from, so the ammonia at the balance is higher at high pressure and at low temperature.
  • The amount made in a single pass is far too small to be worth producing. That is why Haber recycled the unconverted gas.
  • Early in 1909, finely divided osmium gave about 8% ammonia at 175 bar and 600 °C.
  • Mittasch tested more than 2,500 formulas in 6,500 runs by 1910.
  • Test reactors ruptured after only eighty hours.
  • Modern plants run an iron catalyst at 400–500 °C and above 100 bar. 87% of the ammonia made goes to fertilizer.

This account dates the demonstration that convinced BASF to April 1909 — not to July. Source C gives a different date. Both are published. Neither can be preferred from anything in this packet.

E · What the balance does STATUS: documented · established chemistry

N2(g) + 3H2(g) ⇌ 2NH3(g)   gives off heat · about 92 kJ released

Count the molecules. Four molecules of gas go in on the left. Two come out on the right. Squeeze the mixture and the balance eases the only way it can — by making more of the smaller thing. Higher pressure moves the balance toward ammonia.

Now the heat. Because the reaction gives off heat, cooling it moves the balance toward ammonia too — and also makes the reaction slower. You cannot have both.

The catalyst. A catalyst has no effect on where the balance sits. It gives the reaction an easier route, so the balance is reached fast enough to be worth running. It is not used up.

On a single pass about 15 parts in 100 convert. Send the leftover gas round again and again, and the plant converts about 98 parts in 100 overall.

Nitrogen is 78% of the air by volume. Lead melts at 327 °C — remember that number for Task 3.

F · How many people this feeds STATUS: estimated · a calculation by researchers, not a count

Researchers led by J. W. Erisman, writing in Nature Geoscience in 2008, estimated that 44% of the world's population in 2000, and 48% in 2008, depended on food grown with fertilizer made from this reaction.

Nobody has weighed any person's food against the fertilizer that grew it. Other researchers using other methods get other figures — but they all land near one half. It is an estimate. It is not a count.

Teacher Guide

Launch sheet

This case runs over three ordinary class periods. It is the campaign's first technology case, and its central move is one students rarely meet: a reaction can work and still not be a process. Everything in the packet is built to make that distance visible and then to make it explainable.

Before class

Print the eight-page Student Mission or the fourteen-page Accessible Mission. Both carry the whole dossier and every figure, so either packet is completely teachable with no device in the room. Read the reconstruction and attribution notice on learner page 1 yourself first.

Central case resolution

The reaction is reversible and gives off heat, so it settles at a balance holding very little ammonia, and heating it harder makes that worse. Four linked choices make it workable: high pressure (the balance favours the smaller volume), a compromise temperature (yield against rate), a catalyst (rate, not balance), and recycling (the plant, not the pass). Then a second kind of work — catalyst supply, steel, compressors, a plant — turned that into fertilizer. Neither kind of work alone is the answer.

Essential evidence

One number carries the qualification this case exists to make. Lead melts at 327 °C. The process runs at 400–500 °C. The word compromise means between two extremes of this reaction — it does not mean warm, and a student who leaves thinking otherwise has learned the opposite of the lesson. The temperature ladder in 3 · Read the Two Tradeoffs exists for this and Part D assesses it directly.

Likely sticking point

Two, and they pull opposite ways. Most students make the catalyst do the balance's job — it is the single most common error in this topic at any level. A smaller group decides the answer is simply “more pressure” and cannot say why any limit exists. 7 · Weigh Five Claims catches both: Claim 2 is the catalyst error and Claim 3 the temperature one.

What to collect

Collect the whole packet at the end of period 3. 8 · Explain What Made the Process Workable is the graded culminating product; its Part D is the transfer check and uses an unnamed announcement no dossier card answers.

Teacher framing line

“A chemist in 1909 got this reaction to work on a bench the size of your arm. It worked. And it still could not feed one single person, and would not for four more years. Not because he was wrong — because working and useful are different things, and the distance between them is where most of the actual invention happens. By Friday you'll be able to say exactly what that distance was made of, and exactly who crossed it.”

Classroom and technical fallback

If devices fail mid-period, switch to the no-game route and change nothing else. If you lose a period, protect 3 · Read the Two Tradeoffs, 5 · Sort the Work and Put It in Order and 8 · Explain What Made the Process Workable; 1 · Build the Case Vocabulary can be finished as homework. Dropping 6 · Decide What Each Source Can Show removes the source-status evidence from the rubric — that is a modified assessment route, and the source and limitation criterion can no longer be scored.

Lesson overview

Three class periods. Students learn six terms, read one reconstructed laboratory and five real sources including a patent filed in 1909, read a tradeoff panel for direction and for scale, complete a process loop, place four pieces of work with four people across a three-lane technology sequence, sort five kinds of evidence by status, judge five claims including one the published record cannot settle, and write a historical-technological explanation that ends by transferring the test to an unfamiliar announcement.

Measurable objectives

  1. Place all six case terms from an exact-match bank.
  2. Record a provisional explanation of the small yield and name one check it needs.
  3. State which way pressure and temperature each move the balance, name what the temperature is a compromise between, and show from an anchored value that it is not ordinary warmth.
  4. Complete the process loop; state what the catalyst does and does not do, and why a small single-pass conversion is not fatal.
  5. Match four pieces of work to four people and explain why the laboratory result was not yet a process.
  6. Give five kinds of evidence five different statuses and five appropriate limits.
  7. Mark five claims including one the record cannot decide, and name evidence that would move it.
  8. Write a historical-technological explanation covering the problem, the four choices, the two kinds of work, and a transfer.

Success criteria

Students can say why the balance holds so little ammonia and why heating it harder is the wrong move; can name what the operating temperature sits between and state that it is hundreds of degrees; can say what a catalyst does and refuse what it does not do; can explain why the recycle matters without claiming it perfects a single pass; can name what Haber established and what Bosch and BASF solved; and can finish a paragraph about this technology without crediting it to one person.

Two qualifications this case must carry. They are not disclaimers to be read once; they shape the figures, the tasks, the key and the rubric.

One — the temperature. The runtime level describes the operating temperature as warm and as moderate. In ordinary English that means mild, and that reading is wrong by several hundred degrees. Throughout this packet the temperature is a compromise for this reaction's own tradeoff, and the ladder in Task 3 anchors it above the melting point of lead. If you say only one thing about this case, say that.

Two — the attribution. The level ends by crediting the factory to Carl Bosch in a single clause, having credited everything before it to Haber. Neither is false; together they leave a student believing one man invented it and another copied it. The real record names four people doing four different kinds of work, one of whom — Le Rossignol — is a co-inventor on the patent and is left out of the process's usual name. Task 5 is the corrected account and the sequence figure draws its three lanes at equal width for exactly this reason.

Complete teaching procedure / task route — period 1

Three ordinary class periods. This is the route the rubric assesses.

  1. Framing · about 4 minutes. Read the reconstruction and attribution notice on Student page 1 aloud, both paragraphs. The second is the one that matters: this case asks a question about chemistry and a question about people, and the answer to the second is not one name. Do not preview the temperature ladder. Students should meet it as evidence.
  2. 1 · Build the Case Vocabulary · about 8 minutes. Six terms from an exact-match bank, in scrambled order. Do not pre-teach the definitions; the statements carry them.

    Answers, in statement order: yield, equilibrium, catalyst, recycle, compromise, scale up. Statement 3 is the one to read aloud twice — a catalyst changes how fast, not how much at the balance — because Task 4 and Claim 2 both turn on it.

  3. Read the dossier · about 14 minutes. Student pages 2 and 3. Six sources with STATUS lines. Ask the class to read each status line aloud before the card. Put Source A and Source B side by side and ask what kind of thing each one is; that question is the whole of Task 6 row one against row two. On the game route, play Level 4 here instead and then read pages 2 and 3 quickly — they carry the same evidence and considerably more than the level does.
  4. 2 · Record a First Explanation · about 7 minutes. Its directions restrict students to Source A, so it records a starting point rather than a conclusion. Most classes write either “it is leaking” or “he needs more heat” — which are precisely the two wrong diagnoses the runtime level offers as distractors. Do not correct either. Tell them they will come back to this page, and mean it.
  5. Close period 1 · about 4 minutes. Ask one student to read their Task 2 first explanation aloud without comment. Tell the class that tomorrow they find out whether the reaction is broken or just balanced.
Why Task 2 is not corrected. “More heat” is not a misconception to be pre-empted; it is the intuition every chemist of the period shared, and the reason this reaction defeated people for a decade. A class told on day one that heat is wrong learns a slogan. A class that writes “heat it more” and then meets an exothermic balance changes its mind, which is the actual scientific and historical skill.

Formative checks

  • After Task 1, ask what a catalyst does not do. If the class cannot answer, Task 4 Part B will not go well.
  • After Task 3 Part D, scan for any answer that gives a direction without a number. The part asks for a value from the ladder, and it is the audit requirement made assessable.
  • After Task 4 Part B, scan for any answer in which the catalyst produces more ammonia at the balance. That is the error the task exists to catch.
  • After Task 7, scan the Claim 5 column. A class with no question marks anywhere has decided a date that the published record does not settle.

Complete teaching procedure / task route — period 2

  1. 3 · Read the Two Tradeoffs · about 20 minutes. The load-bearing task of the case. Parts A and B are read off the panel: pressure moves the balance toward ammonia; cooling moves it toward ammonia as well. Students are often surprised that both point the same way — and that is the setup for Part C, because the two are not symmetrical. Pressure costs money and danger; cooling costs the reaction itself.

    Part C is the assessed reasoning: the temperature sits between the share of ammonia the balance allows and the speed at which the reaction reaches it. Part D is the qualification made assessable and takes a value from the ladder. Read the ladder aloud from the bottom up and stop on lead. Ask what would happen to a lead spoon in that reactor. The answer is that it would not be a spoon.

  2. 4 · Complete the Process Loop · about 18 minutes. Stage 2 is compression, stage 5 is separating the ammonia as a liquid and drawing it off, stage 6 is returning the unreacted nitrogen and hydrogen to the reactor. Part B must contain both halves: the catalyst makes the reaction reach the balance fast enough to be worth running, and it does not change where the balance sits. Part C must reach the plant rather than the pass: 15 parts in 100 is fatal only if you throw the other 85 away.

    Worth saying aloud: the loop is the invention — not the catalyst, not the pressure, but the decision to stop asking any one pass to do the whole job. That is Haber's, and the patent says so.

  3. Close period 2 · about 3 minutes. Ask the class to look back at their Task 2 answer and put a line through it if they now think it is wrong. Most will.

Formative assessment and access supports

Access supports

The Accessible Mission chunks the dossier across three pages, splits every multi-part task into single actions, and supplies sentence openers. It does not give away any keyed status, name or mark: its scaffolds point at the source or at the kind of thing to look for, never at the answer. The word banks are identical in both editions.

Where students stall

Task 4 stage 5, almost always. Students know ammonia comes out but not how. Point at Source D: the mixture is cooled first, and ammonia turns liquid long before nitrogen and hydrogen do. That is the whole trick.

Scientific and source boundaries

  • No number here is decorative. Every printed value carries a dossier source: the patent's own figures, Travis's and Appl's historical values, and established chemistry for the equilibrium and conversion shares.
  • No curve is drawn. The tradeoff panel gives directions and anchored points only; no source here supplies the values a yield curve would need. Do not let students read quantities off it.
  • The demonstration date is genuinely open. Two published sources in this packet give different dates. That is not an error in the packet; it is the evidence for Task 6 row three and Task 7 Claim 5.
  • The population figure is an estimate — a calculation, not a count. Different methods land near one half; none is a measurement.

Distractor architecture

The wrong answers in this case are not padding. Claim 2 is the catalyst-equilibrium error and is the one most likely to survive teaching. Claim 3 is true about the balance and false about a plant, so a student who answers from chemistry alone gets it wrong — the word that decides it is run. Claim 4 is the attribution collapse and is the game's own framing left uncorrected. Claim 5 is undecidable and is a real disagreement between the two historians in the dossier, printed on the cards with the dates showing.

Why this case does not use CER. The blueprint permits CER and this case declines it. Case dossier · part 2 · two accounts of the same events

C · A historian on who did what STATUS: documented · a historian writing from records

Anthony S. Travis, in a lecture to the Royal Society of Chemistry, published 2015:

  • Haber calculated that about 8% ammonia was reachable at about 600 °C and 200 atmospheres.
  • Robert Le Rossignol, an English assistant, built the steel apparatus and the compressor, and invented the valve that held the pressure. He designed the complete apparatus.
  • Manganese, iron, chromium and nickel gave little. Osmium worked — but only about 100 grams could be bought. That was probably most of the world's supply.
  • Alwin Mittasch at BASF screened catalyst after catalyst until promoted iron worked, in 1910.
  • Carl Bosch and engineer Franz Lappe found that hydrogen was getting into the steel and making it brittle. In February 1911 they answered it with a double-walled converter.
  • Production at Oppau began 9 September 1913 — about twenty tonnes a day, soon about thirty.

On the date of the demonstration that convinced BASF, this historian is careful. He gives 1 July 1909 as one version of events.

D · The industry's own account STATUS: documented · a technical review written for the fertilizer industry

Max Appl, for the International Fertilizer Industry Association, 1997:

  • The reaction gives off heat and takes up less room than the gases it comes from. So the ammonia at the balance is higher at high pressure and at low temperature.
  • The amount made in a single pass is far too small to be worth producing. That is why Haber recycled the leftover gas.
  • Early in 1909, osmium gave about 8% ammonia at 175 bar and 600 °C.
  • Mittasch tested more than 2,500 formulas in 6,500 runs by 1910.
  • Test reactors burst after only eighty hours.
  • Modern plants use an iron catalyst at 400–500 °C, above 100 bar. 87% of the ammonia made goes to fertilizer.

This account dates the demonstration to April 1909 — not July. Source C gives a different date. Both are published. Nothing in this packet decides between them.

Two historians. Same event. Different dates. That is not a mistake in this packet. It is evidence, and you will use it twice — in Task 6 and in Task 7.

E · What the balance does STATUS: documented · established chemistry

N2(g) + 3H2(g) ⇌ 2NH3(g)  gives off heat

1 · Count the molecules. Four go in on the left. Two come out on the right. Squeeze the mixture, and the balance moves toward the smaller thing.

Higher pressure → balance moves toward ammonia.

2 · Now the heat. The reaction gives off heat. So cooling it also moves the balance toward ammonia — and makes the reaction slower. You cannot have both.

Cooling → balance moves toward ammonia, but the reaction gets slow.

3 · The catalyst. A catalyst has no effect on where the balance sits. It gives the reaction an easier route, so the balance is reached fast enough to be worth running. It is not used up.

4 · One pass is not enough. About 15 parts in 100 convert on a single pass. Send the leftover gas round again and again, and the whole plant converts about 98 parts in 100.

Nitrogen is 78% of the air. Lead melts at 327 °C — you will need that number in Task 3.

F · How many people this feeds STATUS: estimated · a calculation, not a count

Researchers led by J. W. Erisman, in Nature Geoscience, 2008, estimated that:

  • 44% of the world's people in 2000, and
  • 48% in 2008,

depended on food grown with fertilizer made from this reaction.

Nobody has weighed any person's food against the fertilizer that grew it. Other researchers get other figures — but they all land near one half. It is an estimate. It is not a count.
