Guiding question

The plains had been through dry years before, many times. Why did these ones take the soil with them?

Reconstruction and causation notice

The Archive puts you on a farm in Oklahoma in 1935 and gives the people there words you can follow. Those scenes are reconstructions. They were written for the game. Nobody in them is a real person and nothing they say is a real thing anybody said. This packet also contains real documents and real records — a law Congress passed that spring, a national weather record, government erosion surveys. Telling those two kinds of source apart is part of the work.

This case has a trap in it, and it is worth naming now. When you find out that ploughing up the grass mattered, it is very easy to swing all the way over and decide the drought did not matter. Both mattered. Neither one of them, on its own, gets you a sky full of soil. Your job is not to pick a winner. It is to say how they worked together, and to say it precisely enough that someone could disagree with you on the evidence.

A · The Archive's plains STATUS: reconstructed · written for the game

The farmer, at a half-buried fence. Before the wheat it was grass — knee-high, roots like wire, all the way down. His grandfather ran cattle on it and never cut a furrow. Then the boom came, grass worth nothing and wheat worth everything, so it was ploughed. All of it, fence to fence, his fields and his neighbours' both, in a few good years. Then the rains quit.

The soil analysis, at the edge of the dead field. The topsoil runs through your fingers like dry flour. Organic matter: a trace. Root mass: none detected. Crumb structure: collapsed. Grains: very fine, and light enough for wind to carry.

The fence line. A ribbon of the old prairie grass survives along the posts, a few feet wide, where the plough could never reach. It is brown and starved for rain — but it is rooted, and the soil around those roots is still there. Step across the fence and the field is bare, cracked and drifted into dunes. Same soil, same sky, the same rain-less years on both sides.

The gully wall. A cut man-deep into what used to be flat field shows the whole profile. The dark topsoil band, the part it took thousands of years to build, should run many inches deep. Here you can cover it with two fingers.

The Archive also sums the case up for you in one line, and that line leans hard: it says the drought only pulled the trigger. Hold on to that phrase and be careful with it. A trigger is still part of how something happens.

B · The drought record STATUS: documented · a national climate record

The National Centers for Environmental Information, the United States government's climate record office, treats the 1930s drought as a single long event.

  • It ran from July 1928 to May 1942.
  • It was the most expansive at its peak of any drought in the record: at its worst it covered at least 60% of the Lower 48 States.
  • The average longest unbroken dry stretch at an individual location was 38.4 months, peaking in March 1935 — the spring this case is set in.

This record measures the weather and nothing else. It cannot tell you why one field blew away while the field on the other side of a fence did not, and on this page the office makes no statement about what caused the drought.

C · What was ploughed, and what the surveys found STATUS: documented · government surveys, reported by a university study

A 2019 study from the University of Illinois collects the figures from the period, including the government's own erosion surveys.

What was ploughed:

  • 104 million acres of native sod ploughed under across the region between 1880 and 1900; 20 million more by 1925; 5 million more by 1930.
  • Roughly 40 million acres ploughed in the southern and parts of the central Great Plains by 1929.

What the Soil Conservation Service surveys found:

  • 1934: 65% of Great Plains acreage had suffered wind-erosion damage; 15% was severely eroded.
  • 1938: 80% of the southern Great Plains affected, 40% seriously.

These are survey assessments reported at second hand, not measurements this packet has inspected. A survey of how much land was damaged cannot, by itself, say how much of the damage to put down to the weather and how much to the ploughing.

Case dossier · part 2 · the law, the model, the argument, the people

D · The law Congress passed STATUS: documented · a real statute, still in the law today

The Soil Conservation Act, approved 27 April 1935. Its own words:

“… the wastage of soil and moisture resources on farm, grazing, and forest lands of the Nation, resulting from soil erosion, is a menace to the national welfare …”

It makes the control and prevention of soil erosion a permanent federal policy, and authorises preventive measures — “engineering operations, methods of cultivation, the growing of vegetation, and changes in use of land”.

A statute records what Congress enacted. It measures no erosion, it names no farm, and it is not by itself evidence about what happened next. Notice what it acts on: the land. It does not act on the weather.

E · A model that puts the two together STATUS: modeled · a computer climate model, not a measurement

Cook, Miller and Seager, in the Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences, 2009, ran climate models of the 1930s.

  • Models driven by the ocean temperatures of the 1930s alone do produce a drought — but the wrong one: centred in the southwest, and missing the warming across the middle of the continent that actually happened.
  • Adding the effect of damaged land — less plant cover, and dust coming off failed fields — was necessary to reproduce what was actually observed.
  • Their conclusion: the damage to the land is likely to have not only contributed to the dust storms, but also amplified the drought itself.

This is a model. It observes no field and records nothing any farmer did. What it establishes is that the ocean-driven drought on its own does not reproduce the 1930s — which is a finding about two causes, not one.

F · Two historians, arguing STATUS: debated · published scholars who weigh this differently

Donald Worster (1979) argues the disaster was made by a system of land use that broke the sod for market wheat. The dust, on his account, followed from that choice.

Geoff Cunfer (2005) worked from county-level farm census records across 450 counties in ten states. He argues land use was steadier than that story implies, that about two-thirds of the Great Plains stayed in unploughed native vegetation, and that drought and heat account for the dust better than the amount of ploughing does.

Read what they are actually arguing about. Neither says the drought did not matter. Neither says land use did not matter. They disagree about how much weight each one carries — and nothing in this packet settles it.

G · Who actually left STATUS: documented · a reference work by a named historian

From the Encyclopedia of Oklahoma History and Culture, Oklahoma Historical Society:

  • Far more migrants left southeastern Oklahoma than left the Dust Bowl region of northwestern Oklahoma and the Panhandle.
  • Net migration loss from Oklahoma across the 1930s may have been as many as 440,000 people.
  • Between 1931 and 1933, 10% of Oklahoma farmers lost their land to foreclosure. More than 60% of Oklahoma farmers were tenants. Machines were consolidating small farms into larger ones, and payments for taking land out of production often meant landowners removing tenants' land from cultivation.

Teacher Guide

Launch sheet

This case runs over three ordinary class periods. It is the campaign's multiple-causation case, and its central move is one that middle-school learners find genuinely hard: holding two causes at once without ranking them. Everything in the packet is built to stop the class from swapping one single-cause story for the opposite single-cause story.

Before class

Print the ten-page Student Mission or the seventeen-page Accessible Mission. Both carry the whole dossier and every figure, so either packet is completely teachable with no device in the room. Read the reconstruction and causation notice on learner page 1 yourself first — its second paragraph is the lesson.

Central case resolution

A long, severe, ocean-driven drought arrived on land whose sod had been broken across millions of acres, leaving it bare and rootless between crops. The drought made the soil dry and loose; the ploughing removed the cover and the roots. Wind erosion needs both, and got both. The conservation response acted on the land, not on the weather — and the rains returned at the same time, so this packet cannot say how much of the ending to credit to it. Four roles, one feedback, no ranking.

Essential evidence

One comparison carries this case. The fence line. Same soil, same sky, the same dry years on both sides, one difference — and two different outcomes. It is the closest thing to a controlled experiment the history offers, and 5 · Read the Fence Line is built so that the learner has to write the same dry years into both columns before they can say what it shows. That identical row is what makes it evidence rather than a slogan.

Likely sticking point

The overcorrection, and it is nearly universal. A class that starts at “it was a drought” will, the moment the fence line lands, swing all the way to “so the drought didn't matter.” Both are single-cause answers and both are wrong. 8 · Weigh Five Claims catches it: Claims 2 and 3 are opposites and both are marked N.

What to collect

Collect the whole packet at the end of period 3. 9 · Explain the Dust Bowl is the graded culminating product; its Part D is the transfer check and asks about an unfamiliar harvest-failure claim that no dossier card answers.

Teacher framing line

“There is a fence in Oklahoma in 1935. On one side of it the ground is bare and blowing away. On the other side, three feet away, the ground is still there. Same soil. Same sky. The same four years without rain. By Friday you'll be able to explain that fence — and here's the hard part: the answer is not the drought, and it is not the plough. Anybody can pick one. Historians get paid to hold both.”

Classroom and technical fallback

If devices fail mid-period, switch to the no-game route and change nothing else. If you lose a period, protect 5 · Read the Fence Line, 6 · Build the Causation Map and 9 · Explain the Dust Bowl; 1 · Build the Case Vocabulary can be finished as homework. Dropping 3 · Locate the Plains and Read the Drought is the one cut that changes the lesson — without it the drought never gets established on its own terms and the class will overcorrect. If you must cut it, that is a modified assessment route and rubric criterion 1 can no longer be scored honestly.

Success criteria

Students can name the drought's span and extent from the record; can say what wind erosion requires and which requirement each cause supplied; can explain what one fence line establishes and what it cannot; can place eight factors in four causal roles and answer both removal tests; can say what the conservation response acted on and what the timeline cannot settle; can state the subsoil's condition as a comparison rather than an absence; and can finish a paragraph about this disaster without giving it a single cause in either direction.

What this case must carry from the audit. Two boundaries. They are not disclaimers to be read once; they shape the figures, the tasks, the key and the rubric.

One — the drought stays a cause. The runtime level's summing-up presses hard in one direction: it says the drought only pulled the trigger, and in one line that it didn't cause this. As rhetoric against the “act of God” excuse of 1935, that is doing honest work. As history handed to a thirteen-year-old, it produces the overcorrection this case exists to prevent. Throughout this packet the drought holds a named causal role, established in Task 3 before land use is mentioned at all, and Task 6's removal test makes a learner say in writing what happens without it. If you say only one thing after gameplay, say that the drought is one of two causes and not a footnote.

Two — the subsoil is degraded, not dead. The Phase 1 audit rejected zero-life and zero-growth claims about the exposed subsoil, and the game was corrected before this package was written: the readings a learner now meets are a trace, far below the range of living topsoil, broken down, and far too little to carry a crop. Every one is a comparison. This packet does not merely avoid the old wording — it teaches the distinction: 4 · Trace How Soil Leaves a Field Part C asks directly what those readings do and do not establish, and Task 8 Claim 4 makes the absolute reading a claim to be refused. A learner who writes that nothing can live below the lost topsoil has gone past the evidence in front of them, and the Answer Key does not accept it at any level.

Complete teaching procedure / task route — period 1

Three ordinary class periods. This is the route the rubric assesses.

  1. Framing · about 4 minutes. Read the reconstruction and causation notice on Student page 1 aloud, both paragraphs. The second is the one that matters. Do not preview the fence line. Students should meet it as evidence.
  2. 1 · Build the Case Vocabulary · about 8 minutes. Six terms from an exact-match bank, in scrambled order.

    Answers, in statement order: topsoil, drought, sod, erosion, contour plowing, shelterbelt. Read statements 1 and 3 aloud together — topsoil and sod — because Tasks 4 and 5 both turn on the difference between what was removed and what was lost.

  3. Read the dossier · about 15 minutes. Student pages 2 and 3. Seven sources with STATUS lines. Ask the class to read each status line aloud before the card. On the game route, play Level 5 here instead and then read pages 2 and 3 quickly — they carry the same evidence and considerably more than the level does, including the drought record and the surveys the level has no equivalent of.
  4. 2 · Record a First Explanation · about 7 minutes. Its directions restrict students to Source A, so it records a starting point rather than a conclusion. Most classes write either “the drought” or “they ploughed it”. Do not correct either. Tell them they will come back to this page, and mean it.
  5. 3 · Locate the Plains and Read the Drought · about 14 minutes. Part A answers: DROUGHT, CORE, PLAINS. Why Task 3 comes before the land-use evidence. Ordering is the argument here. A class that meets the ploughing first hears the drought as the excuse it was used as in 1935, and spends the rest of the case dismissing it. A class that first reads a fourteen-year, continent-wide drought in a government record cannot dismiss it later — the record is in their own handwriting on page 4. That is why the one cut this guide warns against is cutting Task 3.

Complete teaching procedure / task route — period 2

  1. 4 · Trace How Soil Leaves a Field · about 20 minutes. Stage 2 is the surface is dry and loose, the crumb structure collapsed and no roots binding the grains; stage 4 is suspension — the finest particles rise into the air and travel as dust clouds.

    Part B is the interaction made mechanical, and it is worth doing on the board. The ploughing changed condition 2 (cover nonexistent or sparse; condition 3, large fields with no obstructions, is also creditable). The drought changed condition 1 (the soil is loose and dry). Two different causes, two different requirements, and wind erosion needs all four at once. A learner who has written that down cannot then argue that one cause did it alone — the figure will not let them.

    Part C is the subsoil boundary. The readings establish that the subsoil holds much less organic matter and much less biological activity than living topsoil, with broken-down structure, and that it could not carry a crop as it stands. They do not establish that it is empty of life.

  2. 5 · Read the Fence Line · about 18 minutes. The load-bearing task of the case. Insist that the THE DRY YEARS row is filled on both sides, in words, before anyone answers Part B. Students want to leave it blank because it feels like a trick; it is the control, and a comparison with one variable held constant is the only reason this fence is evidence at all.

    Part B: because both sides got the same weather, the difference in outcome has to come from the difference in cover. Part C is the qualification and it has two halves — the fence shows cover mattered here, it does not show the drought was irrelevant (both sides needed dry soil before anything could blow), and one fence is not a region. Source C is what carries it to regional scale: 65% of Great Plains acreage wind-eroded in the 1934 survey.

  3. Close period 2 · about 3 minutes. Ask the class to look back at their Task 2 answer and put a line through it if they now think it is incomplete. Most will. Ask whether “incomplete” and “wrong” are the same thing.

Formative assessment and access supports

Access supports

The Accessible Mission chunks the dossier across four pages, splits every multi-part task onto its own page, and supplies sentence openers. Four declared adaptations: in 5 · Read the Fence Line the COVER row is supplied complete on both sides as a worked model, so an Accessible learner completes eight cells rather than ten; in 6 · Build the Causation Map two of the eight factor cards are pre-placed, one in CONDITION and one in RESPONSE, so six are placed independently; in 7 · Order the Response the event cards carry their dates; and in 8 · Weigh Five Claims each claim carries a pointer to the source that bears on it. All four are supports, not leaks — see the scoring note on page 7.

Where students stall

Task 5, the DRY YEARS row, almost always. They read “what weather this side got” twice, decide it must be a mistake, and skip it. Say out loud: yes, both cells say the same thing, and that is the whole point of the page.

B · How they made the dust. The mechanism was wind erosion, and it needs four things at once: soil that is loose and dry, a surface with no cover on it, open ground with nothing to slow the wind, and wind strong enough to move soil. The drought supplied the first. The ploughing supplied the second. Once both were true the wind lifted the larger grains so they bounced along the surface, knocking more grains loose as they went, and the finest particles rose into the air and travelled as dust clouds.
