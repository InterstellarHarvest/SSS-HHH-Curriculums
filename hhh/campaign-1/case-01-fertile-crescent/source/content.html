Guiding question

Wheat cannot scatter its own seed any more. How did a plant change that much — and what can the evidence actually tell us about how it happened?

Reconstruction notice

The Archive shows you one woman, one field, one season, and gives her words you can follow. That scene is a reconstruction. The Archive has woven generations of harvesters across many valleys into a single person so that the practice can be watched. No record names a first farmer, a first field, or a day when farming began. 9,700 BCE is the year the scene is set in, not a date any find has fixed to an event.

The plant is not invented. The change in the grain is real, it is studied from burnt grain dug out of the ground, and you will work with that evidence in this case.

Write one term from the bank on each line. Use each term once.

WORD BANK Six terms, six lines. Each term is used exactly once. selectionrachisdomesticationcultivationnon-shatteringwild type
  1. The part of a grain stalk that holds the seeds, and that either breaks apart or holds together when the grain ripens, is the
  2. A plant that still scatters its own seed, the way it does without any people, is the
  3. A grain head whose joints hold at ripeness, so the seed stays on the stalk instead of dropping, is

Two of these terms are easy to mix up. One names something people do. The other names something that happens to the plant population, and it can take a very long time. Keep them apart — the whole case turns on the difference.

Case dossier

Four sources. Each one carries a STATUS line. The status is part of the source — read it every time.

A · The Archive's reconstructed field STATUS: reconstructed · a composite scene, not a record

“No record names the first hand that bent wild grain toward harvest — the change took thousands of years and countless hands across many valleys, and the Archive has woven them into one woman, one field, one ordinary act of attention, so the practice itself can be witnessed.”

In the scene, the woman holds up two grain heads side by side: one whole, with every seed still seated on the stalk; one bare and splayed, its joints broken open and its seed already shaken loose to the ground. She walks the rows and closes her hand only on the heads that have held. Those she presses back into the soil.

B · What the Archivist recorded STATUS: reconstructed · observations inside the scene

“She deliberately replants only the grain heads that held their seed instead of shattering, season after season.”

“She sets aside only the non-shattering heads as next year's seed.”

“A visitor leaves carrying the kept seed — the practice crosses to the next settlement.”

Everything on this card comes from inside the Archive's scene. Nothing on it is a record of a real day.

C · Charred grain from excavated sites STATUS: documented · counted from excavated material

When a grain head burns and is buried, the little joints survive as charred fragments. Archaeobotanists can tell a joint that broke apart on its own from one that had to be broken by a person, so they can count how much of a deposit was wild type and how much was non-shattering.

Tanno and Willcox examined 9,844 ancient charred spikelets from four sites in northern Syria and southeastern Turkey. Most were too damaged to read: 804 were identifiable well enough to be sorted into wild type or domesticated type. Among those 804, the early deposits are almost entirely wild type and the later ones hold more and more of the non-shattering kind.

That gradual shift in the proportions is what this evidence shows. It does not name a first farmer, show anyone's intention, or fix a single place where the change began.

D · What researchers still argue about STATUS: debated · interpretation, not measurement

Researchers agree the trait spread because people harvested, kept seed and replanted. They do not agree on how fast, or on exactly which harvesting habits did most of the work.

Models once predicted the trait could take over a field in under a hundred generations. The excavated grain shows something far slower — the non-shattering form takes somewhere between two and four thousand years to become the whole crop. Some researchers argue that regular cutting with a sickle would have pushed harder than the record shows, so other harvesting habits must also have been at work.

Background · outside the reconstruction STATUS: documented · how the plant itself works, from published research

In a wild head, the small joint holding each seed to the stalk snaps at ripeness and throws the seed to the ground, where it sows itself. In a non-shattering head the joints hold, so the seed stays put. A plant that cannot drop its own seed cannot reseed itself. It can only be sown by a hand. This is not part of the Archive's scene — it is how the grain really works, and you will need it from Task 5 onward.

Use Source A and Source B only. Do not use Sources C or D yet.

How to read this timeline

Each bar shows a range of years that archaeologists have dated from digs. Some bars overlap — that means evidence from different places or different crops covers some of the same years. These are not five steps in a row. That overlap is the point: the change built up gradually, in different places at different times, instead of happening all at once.

Teacher Guide

Launch sheet

This is the first full historical case of the program. It runs over two ordinary class periods. Students meet a vivid reconstructed scene, then have to work out what the excavated evidence can and cannot say about it. The reconstruction is not a trap for them to fall into — it is the source they learn to place.

Before class

Print the seven-page Student Mission or the nine-page Accessible Mission. Both carry the full case dossier — Student page 2, Accessible page 3 — and the dated evidence timeline, so either packet is completely teachable with no device in the room. Decide before period 1 which route you are running.

Central historical resolution

Repeated harvesting, seed saving and replanting acted as selection: it kept alive a trait that could not survive in the wild. Across many generations and many regions, non-shattering grain rose from rare to ordinary. No source identifies a first person, a first field, or a starting date, and the change did not run on one clock.

The two routes

Either route assesses the same eight tasks against the same rubric
RouteWhat students doWhat you must still supply
Game routePlay Level 1: watch the woman in the grain field, handle the grain, examine the storage pit, meet the neighbour at the settlement edge, then stabilize the record.Nothing extra. The dossier duplicates the level's evidence, so absent students are not disadvantaged. Read the Reconstruction notice aloud before or after play — the level's own briefing says the same thing.
No-game routeRead the dossier — Student page 2, Accessible page 3 — and work the same eight tasks from it and the timeline.Three minutes of framing. Read the Reconstruction notice on page 1 aloud and say plainly that the woman is a composite.

Essential evidence

Four sources of four different statuses: a reconstruction, counted charred grain, a dated multi-site chronology, and a contested scholarly account. Those four statuses must not collapse into one in 4 · Decide What Each Source Can Carry. The timeline on Student page 3 is the spine of the case; if only one thing survives the two periods, it should be that the scene sits inside the cultivating bar and long before any band where non-shattering grain is common.

Likely sticking point

The story is much easier to hold than the chronology. Students who have played the level will want one woman, one field, one moment. Send them back to the timeline: the bars are thousands of years wide, and there is no bar for a first harvest because nothing excavated identifies one.

What to collect

Collect the whole packet at the end of period 2. 7 · Write the Qualified Explanation is the graded culminating product; 8 · Test a New Claim is the transfer check and uses a site the case never supplied.

Teacher framing line

“You are about to watch one woman change a plant. She is not real — the Archive built her out of thousands of people you will never know the names of. What is real is the grain they left behind, burnt and buried, and by the end of this case you will be able to say exactly how much that grain can prove.”

Classroom and technical fallback

If devices fail mid-period, switch to the no-game route and change nothing else. If you lose a period, protect 3 · Place the Scene on the Evidence Timeline, 5 · Trace the Trait Across Generations and 7 · Write the Qualified Explanation; 1 · Build the Domestication Vocabulary and 6 · Weigh Three Accounts of the Change can be finished as homework. Dropping 4 · Decide What Each Source Can Carry removes the source-status evidence from the rubric — that is a modified assessment route, and the source-status and qualification criterion can no longer be scored.

Lesson overview

Two class periods. Students learn six terms, read a reconstructed harvesting scene and four case sources, place the scene against a dated timeline of excavated evidence, sort the sources by what each can carry, trace how a repeated practice changes a crop, judge three competing accounts, and write a qualified historical explanation. The case is about how a plant population changed and how confidently we can say so — not about naming an inventor.

Guiding historical question

Wheat cannot scatter its own seed any more. How did a plant change that much — and what can the evidence actually tell us about how it happened?

Measurable objectives

  1. Place all six case terms correctly from an exact-match bank.
  2. Locate a reconstructed scene date inside the correct evidence bar and state what that placement rules out.
  3. Give four sources four different statuses and four limits appropriate to those statuses.
  4. Complete a generation trace and explain why the frequency of a trait rises only while the practice continues.
  5. Reject an unsupported account using named evidence, and bound an unresolved question about rate.
  6. Write a qualified historical explanation that uses two sourced pieces of evidence, names one limit, and separates cultivation from domestication in time.
  7. Apply the cultivation and domestication distinction to an unfamiliar dig report.

Success criteria

Students can say what repeated replanting does to a trait that cannot spread on its own; can use the timeline to say why no date can be given for a first domestication; can name something the burnt grain cannot tell us; and can finish an explanation with a qualification rather than a hero.

Academic vocabulary

Six terms, alphabetical. Students meet them in Task 1 and use them through Task 8.
TermClassroom definition
cultivationGrowing plants on purpose — clearing, sowing, tending, harvesting — even while the plants are still the wild kind.
domesticationA lasting change across a plant population that leaves it depending on people to reproduce.
non-shatteringA grain head whose joints hold at ripeness, so the seed stays on the stalk instead of dropping.

selectionOne form of a plant leaving more offspring than another because of which plants get replanted.
wild typeThe form of a plant that still scatters its own seed, as it does without people.

Materials, technology, and preparation

One Student or Accessible packet per learner. Devices are optional. On the game route Level 1 takes roughly fifteen minutes. No preparation beyond printing is required.

Complete teaching procedure / task route — period 1

Two ordinary class periods. This is the route the rubric assesses.

  1. Framing · about 5 minutes. Read the Reconstruction notice on Student page 1 aloud. Say the composite sentence in your own words as well: the Archive built one woman out of thousands of harvesters so the practice could be watched. Students should leave the framing knowing that the woman is invented, the plant is not, and 9,700 BCE is scene setting.
  2. 1 · Build the Domestication Vocabulary · about 10 minutes. Six terms from an exact-match bank. Do not pre-teach the definitions; the statements carry them.

    Answers, in statement order: rachis, wild type, non-shattering, cultivation, selection, domestication. The closing note under the task is the hinge into Task 3 and Task 7 — cultivation is something people do and can start in a single season; domestication is something that happens to a whole population and took thousands of years. Say it out loud if students miss it.

  3. Read the dossier · about 10 minutes. Student page 2, Accessible page 3. Four sources, four STATUS lines. Ask the class to read the status line of each card aloud before reading the card. On the game route, play Level 1 here instead and then read the dossier quickly; the two carry the same evidence.
  4. 2 · Make a First Reading of the Field · about 8 minutes. Students say what the scene shows and name one thing they would need from outside it. Deliberately placed before the excavated evidence arrives, so it records a starting point rather than a conclusion. Accept any genuine outside evidence — dated grain, more than one site, another kind of record. Do not correct a first reading for being incomplete; that is what it is for.
  5. 3 · Place the Scene on the Evidence Timeline · about 20 minutes. The spine of the case, and the heaviest single task. Part A: the scene year, 9,700 BCE, falls inside bar A, which runs from about 9,750 to 8,750 BCE — the bar where people are cultivating grain that is still the wild type. Part B wants any two bars and a statement about domestication developing gradually — slowly, unevenly, or in overlapping stretches. Part C wants bar B, C, D or E used to show that non-shattering grain is not common anywhere until long after 9,700 BCE.

    If a student places the scene in bar B, check whether they read the timeline or the story. Bar B begins about 8,750 BCE — nearly a thousand years after the scene. Point at the dashed 9,700 BCE line rather than telling them the letter.

    Every date on the timeline is a calibrated calendar date, so all five bars are drawn on one scale and can be compared directly. If a student brings in a radiocarbon date from elsewhere, it may be an uncalibrated figure and will not line up; see the source note on the last page.

  6. Close period 1 · about 2 minutes. Ask one student to say what bar A shows. Tell the class that tomorrow they decide what each source is worth.
The timeline is the misconception fix. The single most likely wrong takeaway from this case is that archaeologists documented one woman inventing domesticated wheat in 9,700 BCE. Task 3 exists to make that impossible to hold: the timeline has no bar for a first farmer, and the scene year sits before every bar in which the domesticated form is even present.

Teaching procedure — period 2

  1. 4 · Decide What Each Source Can Carry · about 18 minutes. Students place four statuses from an exact-match bank and then write eight cells — six on the Accessible edition, where the reconstruction row is modeled. The direction line is the one to point at: the four sources do not share a limit.

    Statuses, in row order: reconstructed, documented, inferred, debated. If a student gives all four rows the same limit, the task has not been done — send them back to the STATUS lines in the dossier rather than supplying the answer.

  2. 5 · Trace the Trait Across Generations · about 15 minutes. Stages 1 and 5 are given; students complete the harvest, the store and next year's field, then explain why the frequency rises. This is the mechanism task and it is where the science of the case lives.

    The move students must make is the inversion: in the wild, a head that will not shatter is a dead end, because the plant cannot sow itself. Inside a human practice that exact failure becomes the reason it survives — it is the head still on the stalk when someone comes to gather. Watch for the answer that says the plants “wanted” to change or that the woman “bred” them on purpose. Nobody in the scene is trying to change the plant; the change is a side effect of a practical habit repeated for a very long time.

Two rows may legitimately share a contribution phrase. The four limits may not collapse, because the four statuses are what the task assesses.

  1. 6 · Weigh Three Accounts of the Change · about 12 minutes. Marks: Account 1 N, Account 2 N, Account 3 S. Part B wants named evidence, not a feeling. Part C is the qualification move, and it is worth protecting: the open argument is about rate and mechanism, not about whether people were involved.

    Account 1 is the account the game's own story invites, and rejecting it is not a criticism of the game — the level's briefing says the same thing. Say so if a student feels caught out. Account 2 is the one that sounds scientifically modest and is not: a non-shattering plant cannot sow itself, so without people replanting it the trait disappears rather than spreads.

  2. 7 · Write the Qualified Explanation · about 18 minutes. The culminating product. Four required components are printed on the Student page. Insist on all four, and especially on the fourth: an explanation that never separates cultivation from domestication has not met the case's learning goal, however fluent it is.

    The response this task exists to reject is “the woman domesticated wheat.” It names no mechanism, no source, no limit and no chronology, and it should be sent back rather than scored generously.

  3. 8 · Test a New Claim · about 8 minutes. The transfer check. The site is deliberately outside the case, so no dossier card helps. The report shows cultivation with the domesticated form present but rare — a field being worked, not a domesticated crop. Students who answer with this case's conclusion instead of reading the new report have recalled rather than transferred.
  4. Close · about 3 minutes. Ask one student to read their fourth component aloud. Tell the class that the next case asks what happens to a field several thousand years after this one, when the farming has become a system.

The two periods above are the route the rubric measures: period 1 ends after Task 3, period 2 covers Tasks 4 to 8. If you have only one period, use the fallback priority on page 1 and record which criterion you dropped. Optional game exploration is never part of the assessed route and is never scored.

Why this case does not use Claim–Evidence–Reasoning

The culminating product is an explanation of a process across time, and its hardest requirement is qualification — saying what the evidence cannot reach. A Claim–Evidence–Reasoning frame would push students toward defending a single contested position, and the position here is not seriously contested: everyone agrees people did this. What is contested is speed and mechanism, and that belongs inside the explanation as a limit rather than as the thing being argued. Cases that culminate in a defended interpretation use the shared CER component; this one does not, and that is a deliberate design decision rather than an omission.

Formative checks

  • After Task 1, ask which term names something people do and which names something that happens to a population. If the class cannot answer, Task 7 will not go well.
  • After Task 3, scan Part A for anything other than bar A. That single letter is the case's central misconception check.
  • After Task 4, scan for four identical limits. That is the most common failure in a source-status matrix.
  • After Task 5, ask what would happen to the trait if everyone stopped replanting. A student who says it would carry on has not got the mechanism.

Accessible adaptations actually used

The Accessible Mission is a nine-page packet built alongside the Student Mission, not an enlarged copy. Same eight task numbers, exact titles and accepted answer space; same four-source dossier in shortened form. The adaptations are:

  • 1 · Build the Domestication Vocabulary — the same six-term bank, unchanged, with each statement chunked onto its own line and a larger placement field beneath it.
  • 2 · Make a First Reading of the Field — two labelled fields with sentence starters, so the second half is not lost.
  • 3 · Place the Scene on the Evidence Timeline — the same five bars with the same calibrated dates, and a line beneath the timeline restating the scene year.
  • 4 · Decide What Each Source Can Carry — the reconstruction row is modeled. Students complete three rows, still producing three statuses and three distinct limits.
  • 5 · Trace the Trait Across Generations — stage 2 is worked as a model alongside the fixed first and last stages. Students write stages 3 and 4 and the full explanation, with a starter for the explanation only.
  • 6 · Weigh Three Accounts of the Change — marking unchanged; both written parts carry starters, and Part B names which account to reject so the choice does not consume the reasoning.
  • 7 · Write the Qualified Explanation — the same four required components, split into four labelled fields with starters instead of one open box. The requirement is unchanged; the organisation is supplied.
  • 8 · Test a New Claim — two separate labelled fields instead of one, with the dig report restated in a bulleted list.

Accessible students must still produce four distinct limits in Task 4, the full mechanism explanation in Task 5, and all four components in Task 7. Those are the independent demonstration and are not scaffolded away. Bullets, dictation and scribed responses are acceptable throughout.

Historical and source-status boundaries

The woman, her words, her field and her season are the Archive's reconstruction. How the grain itself works is not: it carries STATUS: documented in its own Background block. The timeline dates are published calibrated findings, given as ranges because that is how they are reported; do not let students copy them as exact events. The mechanism is well supported; how fast it worked, and which harvesting habits mattered most, are open, and Student page 6 says so rather than choosing a side.

If gameplay is unavailable

Nothing changes. The dossier carries all four sources — Student page 2, Accessible page 3 — and the timeline supplies the excavated evidence. No task requires a game state, a score, or a completed level, and none may be graded on any.

Evidence and reasoning architecture

The case runs one reasoning operation — inference from a trait to a practice, held against a chronology — through four sources of four statuses. The reasoning path is: the trait cannot spread on its own (mechanism) → it did spread (counted grain) → therefore something kept sowing it (inference) → but slowly and unevenly (chronology) → therefore no single moment, place or person can be named (limit). Every task after the vocabulary sits somewhere on that path.

The competing accounts in Task 6 are not decoration. Account 1 is the story the game tells and the one students arrive with; Account 2 is the over-correction students reach for once they learn to distrust a narrative. Both are rejected by evidence, and rejecting the second is the harder move.

Completed exemplar. Three organizer stages and the explanation are all required.

Stage 2 · At the harvest — the heads that shattered have already dropped their seed on the ground, so what ends up in her hands is weighted toward the heads that held. The rare non-shattering head is over-represented in the harvest compared with how common it is in the field.

Stage 3 · In the storage pit — she sets aside for seed the heads that are still whole and still holding their grain — that is, the non-shattering ones. The pit ends up holding a much higher share of that form than the wild field does.

Stage 4 · In next year's field — the crop grows from the seed she sowed, so a larger share of it is non-shattering than in the field she first harvested. Then the same harvest happens again to this new, already-shifted crop.

Why repeating it raises how common the trait is — each cycle passes on seed from a group that was already tilted toward the non-shattering form, so the tilt gets a little bigger every generation and never resets. Nobody is trying to change the plant; the change is a side effect of a sensible habit repeated for a very long time. The trait cannot spread by itself, because a head that will not shatter cannot drop its own seed. If people stopped replanting it, it would not simply stay put — it would die out, because that form has no way of sowing itself.

Acceptable variation. Wording is open and bullets are acceptable. Proficient work must contain two things: that the seed sown each year is not a fair sample of the field, and that the trait depends on people continuing. A response that describes the three stages accurately but never says why the frequency rises is developing, not proficient. A response saying the plant adapted, or that the woman bred it on purpose, has the mechanism wrong even if the stages are filled in correctly.
