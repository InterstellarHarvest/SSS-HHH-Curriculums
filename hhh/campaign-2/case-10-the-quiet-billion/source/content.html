How should historians explain the 1960s wheat gains when the numbers rose, improved varieties mattered, several conditions changed at once, and both the strongest success story and the strongest failure story claim more than the evidence can prove?

Source-status notice

This packet holds three kinds of material, and telling them apart is part of the work.

  • RECONSTRUCTED GAME EVIDENCE — Sources A to E, written for the game, along with every number, name and reading inside them. They may be reasoned about, compared and tested. They may never be quoted as facts about the real world.
  • DOCUMENTED — Sources F to I. Real published sources: an official national crop record, a participant's own 1970 lecture, a wheat research centre's account of its own history, and a retrospective written four decades later.
  • CURRICULUM-ORIGINAL FIGURE — Figures 1, 2 and 3, drawn for this packet from the documented sources. A figure organises evidence. It is not itself evidence.

The rule that joins them: Reconstructed game evidence can support a judgment about the game's record. It cannot establish a real historical claim. Documented historical evidence can support claims about the Green Revolution. It cannot prove that a game record is genuine.

About the title. The title comes from the game. Treat “a billion lives” as a claim that would require a stated method and counterfactual, not as a measurement supplied by this packet.

B · The two wheats SOURCE STATUS · RECONSTRUCTED GAME EVIDENCE

Two plots sit side by side under the plot-marker stakes. On the left, tall wheat is chest-high and most of it is bent flat to the ground under the weight of its own grain. On the right, the same heavy heads sit on short, stiff stalks barely past your hips, standing upright. Same grain, same field, same weather.

EXTRACT — TAA TRAIT SCAN, comparative plots
tall variety stem ~120 cm - LODGED under the grain load - crop lost semi-dwarf stem ~90 cm - stiff - STANDING under the identical load disease the semi-dwarf line here also carries stem-rust resistance; the tall line does not fertiliser both take nitrogen - only the short straw survives the heavier head it makes

Two separate traits, carried by one plant. Short stiff straw answers lodging. Rust resistance answers a fungus. Breeding put both into one variety; neither trait produces the other, and a tall wheat can be rust-resistant. An invented reading: it settles a question inside the case and no question outside it.

The station's worktable is buried under a decade of paper: seed envelopes, yield notebooks, and a hand-drawn pedigree chart pinned to the wall, tracing every released variety back through its crosses to the dwarfing stock at the root. Every branch is dated.

EXTRACT — TAA CROSS-REFERENCE, pedigree and harvest record

D · Dr. Rao, the deployment agronomist SOURCE STATUS · RECONSTRUCTED GAME EVIDENCE

Who gives it. Dr. Rao, an agronomist standing at the crop's edge with a clipboard while a line of harvesters moves through wheat that runs out of sight. He was written for the game.

  • He ran the deployment in these fields and counted the harvests himself.
  • The old wheat gave a fixed amount per acre and no more, and in a bad year gave hunger. The new wheat near-doubled it, and held through the season.
  • Convincing farmers was hard at first: a farmer plants what fed him last year. Demonstration plots were sown beside the farmers' own fields and the two crops were left to answer for themselves.
  • Whatever paper says the wheat failed, he says, was not written by anyone who stood in the field at the cutting.

Testimony written for the game. Dr. Rao is not a real witness and his yield figure is not a measurement. What he says can be tested against the other game evidence; it cannot establish anything about a real field, a real harvest or a real farmer.

E · The Failure Report SOURCE STATUS · RECONSTRUCTED GAME EVIDENCE

What it claims. A trial-failure summary, stamped and filed: the semi-dwarf lines lodged in the wind and were quietly abandoned; the whole programme was a well-funded disappointment buried to spare the embarrassment. The documented record · four real published sources

F · The India wheat record, 1964–65 to 1969–70 SOURCE STATUS · DOCUMENTED

Where it comes from. The Government of India's official agricultural statistics for wheat: for each crop year, the area sown, the production harvested, the yield per hectare and the irrigated share of the wheat area. The six-year series is printed in full in Figure 1, on page 5.

It establishes what was sown, harvested, produced per hectare and irrigated, year by year. It explains nothing, and says nothing about hunger, welfare or lives.

G · Borlaug's Nobel lecture, 11 December 1970 SOURCE STATUS · DOCUMENTED

Where it comes from. The lecture Norman Borlaug gave on receiving the 1970 Nobel Peace Prize. He ran the Mexican wheat programme, so this is a participant's own account, not a neutral outside assessment.

  • As fertiliser use pushed Mexican yields up, lodging — his gloss: “falling over of the plant” — began to limit further increases. A search for genetic dwarfness followed, and Norin 10, an extremely dwarf wheat from Japan, proved to be a suitable source. Crosses began in 1954; the dwarf Mexican wheats were first distributed in 1961.
  • He names four things together in those varieties: high yield potential, short straw, a strong and efficient response to heavy fertiliser, and a broad spectrum of disease resistance.
  • “This rapid increase in wheat production was not based solely on the use of Mexican dwarf varieties”: it involved a whole new production technology — and, equally important, government policy assuring the farmer a fair price, the inputs available, and the credit with which to buy them.
  • Seed: 350 tons to Pakistan and 250 to India in 1965 for on-farm testing; 18,000 tons to India in 1966, 42,000 to Pakistan a year later.
  • He states his own limits. “There are no miracles in agricultural production. Nor is there such a thing as a miracle variety.” The increase had so far been in irrigated areas, and not all farmers even there had adopted or benefited. And: “The green revolution has won a temporary success in man's war against hunger and deprivation; it has given man a breathing space.”

A participant with a case to make — whose own lecture refuses the strongest version of it.

H · CIMMYT on where the dwarfing came from SOURCE STATUS · DOCUMENTED

Where it comes from. The International Maize and Wheat Improvement Center (CIMMYT), the wheat research centre in Mexico, writing on its own history.

  • In 1935 the Japanese scientist Gonjoro Inazuka crossed a semi-dwarf Japanese landrace with two American varieties. The result was Norin 10, which brought wheat height down from about 150 cm to 60–110 cm through the reduced-height genes Rht1 and Rht2.
  • S. D. Salmon of the United States Department of Agriculture took samples home, where in the late 1940s Orville Vogel at Washington State University bred semi-dwarf winter wheats from them. Gaines was the first.
  • Borlaug's team in Mexico were working on two problems, lodging and rust resistance. He wrote to Vogel for seed carrying the dwarfing genes; crossing began in 1953, the first attempt failed, and the result was a short, stiff-strawed spring wheat less likely to lodge. Within seven years Mexico's average yields had doubled.
  • CIMMYT describes the start of the Green Revolution as the combining of three separate traits — semi-dwarf, rust resistant, and photoperiod insensitive. One of the crosses it pictures is a tall variety that was resistant to stem rust.
  • Trials in India and Pakistan produced high yields “but only after agronomy practices were changed. Without these changes, the Green Revolution would never have taken off.”

Dwarfing and rust resistance were two goals combined by breeding, not one trait. Nothing here is about the game, and no count of lives.

I · A retrospective, forty years later SOURCE STATUS · DOCUMENTED

Where it comes from. Prabhu L. Pingali, “Green Revolution: Impacts, limits, and the path ahead”, Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences, 2012 — a review written four decades later.

  • Across about fifty years, cereal production in the developing world tripled on only about 30 per cent more land, while populations more than doubled.
  • “Much of the success was caused by the combination of high rates of investment in crop research, infrastructure, and market development and appropriate policy support.” Fertiliser and irrigation were critical components, and Asia had already invested heavily in irrigation before the period began.
  • Between 1960 and 2000, wheat yields across developing countries rose 208 per cent. Crop germplasm improvement alone accounts for about 1.0 per cent a year.
  • The benefits were uneven: by 1998 modern varieties covered 82 per cent of the area in Asian countries and 27 per cent in Africa; sub-Saharan Africa is the exception to the global trend; women farmers gained proportionally less. Calories rose, but micronutrient malnutrition persisted, and environmental impacts were mixed — land spared from clearing, against unintended consequences in water use, soil damage and chemical run-off that the article blames on subsidised over-use rather than on the technology.
  • It reports estimates that without the crop-improvement programmes, food production in developing countries would have been almost 20 per cent lower — an estimate with a stated method, and about production, not about lives.

It counts no lives saved, and it settles no question about the game's records.

Launch sheet

Students decide what the 1960s wheat gains actually show. Indian wheat production rose sharply between 1964–65 and 1969–70, improved semi-dwarf varieties genuinely mattered, and several other conditions changed in the same years. Two strong stories both claim more than the evidence carries: the buried Failure Report says the wheat failed, and the popular success story says one seed saved a billion lives. The curriculum's conclusion is more qualified than the game's.

Before class

Print the eight-page Student Mission or the ten-page Accessible Mission. Either packet carries the whole reconstructed dossier, all four documented source cards and all three figures, so either is completely teachable with no device in the room. Read the source-status notice on learner page 1 yourself first, and plan to read it aloud — including the sentence about the title.

The one thing to say out loud on day one

“A billion lives” is the game's phrase, and it is a claim, not a measurement. Nothing in this packet counts lives, and no task asks a student to. A class that finishes this unit able to recite “one seed saved a billion people” has learned the wrong lesson, however well it answers everything else.

Essential evidence

Four documented sources carry this case and they carry different parts of it. Source F, the official India wheat record, supplies the numbers and nothing else: it says what was sown, harvested and irrigated, and explains none of it. Source G, Borlaug's own 1970 Nobel lecture, supplies the mechanism, the seed movement and — from a participant — the refusal of the strongest claim. Source H, CIMMYT, supplies the route the dwarfing gene travelled and the fact that dwarfing and rust resistance were two goals combined. Source I, Pingali's 2012 retrospective, supplies scale, unevenness and cost. 4 · Read the Numbers Carefully rests on F; 5 · Trace the Wheat and the Package on G and H; 7 · Test the Competing Interpretations and 8 · Write a Qualified Historical Finding on all four.

Likely sticking point

Two, and they pull in opposite directions. Students read a rising production line and conclude that the new seed alone caused all of it. Or they meet the environmental and distributional costs and conclude the gains were not real. 4 · Read the Numbers Carefully and 5 · Trace the Wheat and the Package exist to close both: the numbers are real, several things changed at once, and no source here measures a share.

What to collect

Collect the whole packet at the end. 8 · Write a Qualified Historical Finding is the graded culminating product; it requires a finding, one quantitative value with units and scope, a second documented source, other contributing conditions, one explicit limit, and the next evidence needed. 2 · Set the Claim Test is provisional and is read rather than marked.

Teacher framing line

“In six years, India's wheat harvest went from about twelve million tonnes to about twenty. That happened. Now: a buried report in the archive says the new wheat failed, and a famous line says the new wheat saved a billion lives. This week you are going to do two things with that. First, read the numbers carefully enough to see what actually changed — and how many things changed at once. Second, work out which claims the evidence can carry, and which ones are bigger than anything anyone here has measured.”

Classroom and technical fallback

If devices fail mid-period, switch to the no-game route and change nothing else. If you lose time, protect 4 · Read the Numbers Carefully, 7 · Test the Competing Interpretations and 8 · Write a Qualified Historical Finding; 1 · Build the Case Vocabulary can be finished as homework and 3 · Keep the Evidence Layers Separate can be worked in pairs. Complete teaching procedure

The assessed core route is sixty minutes of task time — the eight tasks below, at the minutes printed against each. Around that core the packet also needs about twenty minutes for launch, reading and close, which are listed here as unnumbered steps. In an ordinary fifty-minute period that is two periods; in a block period it is one. Do not attempt to insert gameplay into the sixty minutes. The level's own evidence route is a substantial read on top of play, and a class taking the game route should play in a separate period and arrive at the packet having already met Sources A to E.

  1. Launch — 3 min. Read the source-status notice on learner page 1 aloud, including the sentence about the title. Say the rule in your own words: five of the sources were written for a game, four are real published sources, three are figures drawn for these pages, and none of them proves anything about another kind.
  2. 1 · Build the Case Vocabulary — 5 min. Nine terms with their definitions printed, seven applications. There is no word bank and none is needed: the terms are on the page. Circulate for one thing only — whether yield has landed as a per-hectare measure, because Task 4 turns on it.
  3. Read Sources A to E — 8 min. Learner pages 2 and 3. Say once, before they start, that all five were written for the game, that Dr. Rao is not a real agronomist, and that his yield figure is not a measurement.
  4. 2 · Set the Claim Test — 4 min. Provisional, and deliberately taken before the documented sources arrive. There is no correct answer and none is keyed. Read the three slots as a diagnostic: a class that asks only “is there a source?” has not yet thought about method, comparison or denominator, and that is the useful gap the rest of the case fills.
  5. Read Sources F to I — 6 min. Learner page 4. Read aloud the two sentences in Source G where Borlaug refuses the strongest version of his own story: there are no miracles, and the revolution has won a temporary success. They do more work than any teacher warning can.
  6. 3 · Keep the Evidence Layers Separate — 5 min. Six cells and one boundary sentence. Do the third row together, out loud: a figure organises evidence and establishes nothing on its own. Then release the other two.
  7. 4 · Read the Numbers Carefully — 10 min. The load-bearing task. Do read A2 together and stop there: the year production rose most is 1967–68, and the next two reads show that in that same year the area sown rose and the irrigated share fell. That pairing is the whole lesson; do not give it away before they find it.
  8. 5 · Trace the Wheat and the Package — 8 min. Route first, package second. Watch for a route box that says only a place name; the answer has to say what that place added. Part C must use the term input package.
  9. 6 · Test the Failure Report — 8 min. Say once, before they start, that the four tests use Sources A to D only and that the paperwork is not one of them. The commonest failure is a verdict reached from the letterhead — in either direction.
  10. 7 · Test the Competing Interpretations — 8 min. Insist on the first column for every row, including the two that are wrong. An interpretation that had no valid evidence at all would not be worth testing, and finding the true part of a wrong claim is the skill.

The two boundaries this case exists to hold

Production, yield, hunger, welfare and lives saved are five different things

Production is the total harvested: 16.54 million tonnes in 1967–68. Yield is production per unit of area: 1,103 kg/ha the same year. Production can rise from yield, from area, or both — and in 1967–68 both rose. Hunger is who went without, which no harvest figure reports; welfare depends on distribution, prices, incomes and diet. A count of lives saved is a counterfactual needing a method, a population, a period, a comparison group and a denominator. Each step is a separate claim, and nothing here supplies the last.

Semi-dwarfing and lodging resistance are not rust resistance

Semi-dwarfing is short, stiff straw, and it answers lodging: a well-fed plant falling flat under its own head. Rust resistance answers a fungal disease and is a different trait with different genes. Varieties carried both because breeders selected for both — CIMMYT calls the start of the Green Revolution a combining of three separate traits, and the tall Chapingo 53, a parent pictured in its own account, was itself rust-resistant. Do not let a class leave believing that making the plant short is what made it resist disease.

Measurement first: four measures, all moving in Figure 1. The competing records

Inside the game the Failure Report fails on four tests: it says the lines lodged, and the scan shows the short straw standing under an identical load; it says the trials were abandoned, and the pedigree runs unbroken with rising yields; it says the programme was a disappointment, and the field testimony describes a harvest counted in person; and it stands alone against three sources that agree. G · Borlaug's Nobel lecture, 11 December 1970 SOURCE STATUS · DOCUMENTED

Where it comes from. The lecture Norman Borlaug gave on receiving the 1970 Nobel Peace Prize. He ran the Mexican wheat programme, so this is a participant's own account, not a neutral outside judgment.

The problem, and the fix.

  • As fertiliser use pushed Mexican yields up, lodging — “falling over of the plant” — began to limit any further increase.
  • A search was made for a source of genetic dwarfness. Norin 10, an extremely dwarf wheat from Japan, proved to be a suitable source. Crosses began in 1954. The dwarf Mexican wheats were first distributed in Mexico in 1961.
  • He names four things together in those varieties: high yield potential, short straw, a strong and efficient response to heavy fertiliser, and a broad spectrum of disease resistance.

What else it took.

  • “This rapid increase in wheat production was not based solely on the use of Mexican dwarf varieties.” It involved a whole new production technology.
  • Equally important, he says, was government economic policy: a fair assured price for the grain, the inputs available — seed, fertilisers, weed killers, machinery — and the credit to buy them.
  • Seed moved in 1965 (350 tons to Pakistan, 250 to India), then 18,000 tons to India in 1966 and 42,000 tons to Pakistan a year later.

His own limits.

  • “There are no miracles in agricultural production. Nor is there such a thing as a miracle variety.”
  • The increase had so far been in irrigated areas, and not all farmers even there had adopted or benefited from the new seed.
  • “The green revolution has won a temporary success in man's war against hunger and deprivation; it has given man a breathing space.”

A participant with a case to make — whose own lecture refuses the strongest version of it.

H · CIMMYT on where the dwarfing came from SOURCE STATUS · DOCUMENTED

Where it comes from. The International Maize and Wheat Improvement Center (CIMMYT), the wheat research centre in Mexico, writing on its own history.

  • In 1935 the Japanese scientist Gonjoro Inazuka crossed a semi-dwarf Japanese landrace with two American varieties: Norin 10, which brought wheat height down from about 150 cm to 60–110 cm through the reduced-height genes Rht1 and Rht2.
  • S. D. Salmon of the USDA took samples home; in the late 1940s Orville Vogel bred semi-dwarf winter wheats from them at Washington State University.
  • Borlaug's team in Mexico were working on two problems: lodging and rust resistance. He wrote to Vogel for seed carrying the dwarfing genes; crossing began in 1953, the first attempt failed, and the result was a short, stiff-strawed spring wheat less likely to lodge. Mexico's average yields doubled within seven years.
  • CIMMYT describes the start of the Green Revolution as the combining of three separate traits: semi-dwarf, rust resistant, and photoperiod insensitive. One of the crosses it pictures is a tall variety that resisted stem rust.
  • Trials in India and Pakistan gave high yields “but only after agronomy practices were changed. Without these changes, the Green Revolution would never have taken off.”

Dwarfing and rust resistance were two goals combined by breeding, not one trait. Nothing about the game; no count of lives.
