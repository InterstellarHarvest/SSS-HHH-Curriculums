The case file · what the Archive reconstructed

Five sources make the case inside the game. All five were written for the game. Read the SOURCE STATUS line before you read the card.

A · The farmer at the plots SOURCE STATUS · RECONSTRUCTED GAME EVIDENCE

Who gives it. Xochitl, a farmer working a plot at the water's edge.

  • The plots give several harvests in a year and are never left to rest — maize, then beans, then squash, then amaranth.
  • Each season new mud is lifted from the canal floor and laid on top of the plot.
  • Her grandmother worked the same plot, and it was not new then.

Inside the case this supports a system that is producing and being maintained. It establishes nothing about any real plot.

B · The plot soil SOURCE STATUS · RECONSTRUCTED GAME EVIDENCE

EXTRACT — FIELD SURVEY
SURFACE TO 40 CM composition layered lake mud, decaying green matter nutrient cycle ACTIVE, continuously replenished salt crust none present

A reading invented for the case. It describes one reconstructed plot and no real soil anywhere.

C · The lake works SOURCE STATUS · RECONSTRUCTED GAME EVIDENCE

  • Seen from the causeway, the lake is not simply water. A long earthwork crosses the open water, pierced by wooden gates.
  • A gate crew works one of the sluices, timing it against the wind.
  • On the garden side the water is clear; beyond the earthwork it is brackish. The two are held apart on purpose.

Inside the case this shows water being managed by design. It is not a survey of the real basin and it fixes no real arrangement of the lake.

D · The harvest count SOURCE STATUS · RECONSTRUCTED GAME EVIDENCE

  • A painted screenfold ledger: named plots, counted seasons, running totals, generations deep, from the same gardens, with yields steady to rising.
  • How the count was made. The record-keeper counts each harvest three times — at the plot, at the canoe and at the market — and paints only what all three agree on.

An invented document. Nothing here establishes that such a record survives, or what a surviving one would say.

E · The buried collapse account SOURCE STATUS · RECONSTRUCTED GAME EVIDENCE

What it claims. That salt worked its way into the plots, that the canals fouled, and that the gardens finally exhausted the valley that fed them.

What can be seen in it. It is finely made. It names no plot, no season and no keeper. Every other record in this case names its plots.

This is the claim the case exists to test. It is invented, and so is everything it would have to be tested against inside the game.

The real-world record · the published plan

F · The 1524 published plan of the lake city SOURCE STATUS · HISTORICAL MAP

What it is. The first published plan of Tenochtitlan, printed at Nuremberg by Friedrich Peypus in 1524 inside the first Latin edition of Hernán Cortés's second letter to Charles V. The Library of Congress holds the digitised copy.

What the Library's record of it says.

  • Tenochtitlan was founded in the fourteenth century on an island in the salt lake of Texcoco.
  • Wide causeways connect the island city to the shores of the lake.
  • The plan is oriented with west at the top, and labels the city Temixtitan.
  • Cortés's letter is dated 30 October 1520, and the plan depicts the city in that year.

The limit, in plain words. The plan was published in 1524 and depicts the city about 1520. It is not a picture of the city in 1487. It was printed in Europe, in a book by the commander whose army attacked and destroyed the city in 1521, more than thirty years after the year this case is set in.

The real-world record · the system and the archaeology

G · How the system is built and worked SOURCE STATUS · DOCUMENTED

Where it comes from. The Food and Agriculture Organization of the United Nations, which designated the Chinampas Agricultural System of Mexico City a Globally Important Agricultural Heritage System in 2017.

  • Chinampas are raised fields built up in shallow water from lake sediment, branches and decaying vegetation, in small islands in strips, with a web of channels between them.
  • Building one runs in steps: stake out the perimeter with cut branches and stakes of ahuejote, a native willow; build a structure around the stakes; then form the soil by filling with two layers, one of organic matter and one of lake sludge.
  • Rows of ahuejote along the edges perform several functions in the system.
  • Soil fertility is kept up by constant additions of organic matter, in particular aquatic vegetation taken from the water.
  • The channels are part of the irrigation system, average about 1.5 metres deep, and between 4 and 6 metres wide where they are used for transport by canoe.
  • The channels also carry away excess water in the rainy season, working as vessels of regulation.
  • The method is an intensive one, allowing cultivation throughout the year, and it is still worked today in the lake area of Xochimilco.
  • A note on the name. The same FAO summary also describes the system as floating artificial islands. “Floating gardens” is a conventional nickname. The construction steps in that same document describe fields built up from the lake bottom on a staked structure.

It documents the system as it is worked and studied now. It is not a record of any particular year and it establishes nothing about the game's case.

H · What has been dug up, and what was built to hold the water SOURCE STATUS · DOCUMENTED

Where it comes from. Mexico's Instituto Nacional de Antropología e Historia (INAH).

The archaeology. INAH's January 2024 bulletin on the Arqueología Mexicana issue devoted to the chinampas of the Basin of Mexico reports that the practice is far older than the Mexica period: the earliest chinampas found in the basin belong to the Early Postclassic, 900 to 1200 CE, at Xaltocan, and the method was taken up by peoples around the basin's lakes. An INAH salvage excavation in 2015 in the Cuauhtémoc borough of Mexico City identified remains of chinampas and canals in use between 1300 and 1521 CE, in what had been a Mexica district of ancient México-Tenochtitlan. Study of plant remains and sediments from such contexts records maize, squash, chia, chile, amaranth, purslane, tomatillo and nopal.

The water works. INAH's record of the Albarradón de San Cristóbal at Ecatepec describes pre-Hispanic hydraulic infrastructure in the basin: a dike built as part of the works against flooding at Tenochtitlan, to separate the waters within Lake Texcoco and reduce the chance of the city flooding. INAH attributes it to a joint effort of the Mexica ruler Moctezuma Ilhuicamina and Nezahualcóyotl of Texcoco. The same record notes that the causeway was destroyed on Cortés's orders, rebuilt after a severe flood in 1604, and then renewed, rebuilt, restored and reinforced again in 1675, 1692, 1743 and 1856.

Archaeology establishes that chinampas and canals were built and used, and that the basin's water was managed with dikes. It supplies nobody's words, it confirms no event in the game, and INAH's account does not say which part of the lake held which water.

Teacher Guide

Launch sheet

This case runs over three ordinary class periods. Students explain how an engineered wetland farming system worked, and then say what each kind of source can and cannot establish about it. The chinampa system is real and is still farmed. The 1487 scene, the people in it and the painted record are reconstructions written for the game.

Before class

Print the eight-page Student Mission or the ten-page Accessible Mission. Either packet carries the whole reconstructed dossier, all three real-world source cards and both figures, so either is completely teachable with no device in the room. Read the source-status notice on learner page 1 yourself first, and plan to read it aloud.

The one thing to say out loud on day one

The English nickname is misleading and the packet says so in print. Chinampas are raised fields, built up in shallow water from lake sediment, branches and decaying vegetation on a staked structure set into the lake bottom. They do not float, and a class that leaves this unit believing chinampas were rafts has learned the wrong thing however well it answers everything else.

Essential evidence

Two real-world sources carry this case, and they carry different halves of it. The FAO documentation supplies the physical system: the staked perimeter, the two soil layers, the canal, the willow, the organic matter lifted from the water, the year-round intensive cropping. INAH supplies the depth and the scale: chinampas and canals in the Basin of Mexico from the Early Postclassic onward, remains in use between 1300 and 1521 CE in what was a district of Tenochtitlan, and a dike built to separate the waters of Lake Texcoco and reduce flooding. 4 · Trace the Chinampa System at Two Scales and 7 · Explain the Engineered Landscape are built so that a learner has to put both halves together.

Likely sticking point

The nickname does the damage before the lesson starts, and the FAO summary itself uses the phrase “floating artificial islands”. Students read that and conclude the fields floated. 4 · Trace the Chinampa System at Two Scales exists to close it: the figure draws the lake bottom under the field and prints the sentence that the field is built up from it and does not float.

What to collect

Collect the whole packet at the end of period 3. 7 · Explain the Engineered Landscape is the graded culminating product; it requires two evidence links from different real-world sources and one explicit source qualification. 8 · Transfer the Method is the transfer check, answered about a place the packet never mentions.

Teacher framing line

“Somebody built farmland on a lake, and fed a city from it for generations. This week you are going to work out how — how the ground was made, how the water was kept where it was wanted, and how the same plot could be cropped four times a year without wearing out. And then you are going to do the harder half: say which of the things you know come from a real published record, and which come from a story a game told you.”

Classroom and technical fallback

If devices fail mid-period, switch to the no-game route and change nothing else. If you lose a period, protect 4 · Trace the Chinampa System at Two Scales, 5 · Compare What the Sources Can Establish and 7 · Explain the Engineered Landscape; 1 · Build the Case Vocabulary can be finished as homework and 3 · Read the Lake-City Map can be worked in pairs. Dropping 4 · Trace the Chinampa System at Two Scales is the one cut that breaks the lesson, because it is the only place the physical system is built rather than named.

Lesson overview

Three class periods. Students learn six terms, write down a provisional geographic test before the explanatory sources arrive, read five reconstructed case records and three real-world source cards under printed status lines, read a sourced historical map and state a limit on it, trace one agricultural system at two scales, decide what four kinds of source contribute and what none of them can establish alone, separate what the game's evidence shows from what the real record shows, write an engineered-landscape explanation with two sourced links and one stated limit, and apply the same two checks to an unfamiliar report about somewhere else.

Measurable objectives

  • Apply six terms to the parts and relationships of a wetland raised-field system.
  • Read a sourced historical map for the relationship between a settlement, a body of water and its connections to land, and state one limitation of that map.
  • Explain how field construction, canals, soil renewal, intensive cropping and basin-scale water management work on one another.
  • State for each of four kinds of source what it contributes and what it cannot establish alone.
  • Keep reconstructed case evidence and documented real-world evidence in separate layers when testing a claim.
  • Write an explanation of an engineered landscape using at least two real-world sources and one explicit source qualification.
  • Name a physical check and a source check that would test an unfamiliar claim about another wetland system.

Complete teaching procedure

Three periods of about fifty minutes. Every numbered Student task appears below in the order the packet prints it. If your periods are shorter, move the reading in period 1 to homework rather than cutting a task.

  1. Period 1 · Launch — 6 min. Read the source-status notice on learner page 1 aloud. Say the rule in your own words: five of the sources were written for a game, three are real published records, and neither kind proves anything about the other. Post the guiding question.
  2. Period 1 · 1 · Build the Case Vocabulary — 8 min. Exact-match bank, six terms, six statements, no decoys. Circulate for one thing only: whether raised field and chinampa have landed as a general kind and a particular case of it rather than as two names for the same thing.
  3. Period 1 · Read Sources A to E — 12 min. Learner page 2. Read Source A aloud; let students read B to E. Say once, before they start, that all five were written for the game.
  4. Period 1 · 2 · Set a Geographic Test — 8 min. Provisional, and deliberately taken before the explanatory sources arrive. There is no correct answer and none is keyed. Read the two slots as a diagnostic: a class that names only things visible on the surface has not yet thought about water, and that is the useful gap the next two pages fill.
  5. Period 1 · Read Sources F, G and H — 14 min. Learner pages 3 and 4. Read the last bullet of Source G aloud — the note on the name. It is the sentence the rest of the case turns on.
  6. Period 2 · 3 · Read the Lake-City Map — 16 min. Part D is the one to protect. A learner who writes that the map is old has not stated a limitation; a learner who writes that it was published in 1524, depicts about 1520, and was printed in Europe by the conquering side has.
  7. Period 2 · 4 · Trace the Chinampa System at Two Scales — 20 min. The load-bearing task. Do Part A together as a class, out loud, before releasing the rest — the answer has to reach the lake bottom, or the misconception survives the lesson.
  8. Period 3 · 5 · Compare What the Sources Can Establish — 16 min. Eight fields. Work row A together, then release. Watch for a class treating all four kinds as equally strong; the reconstructed row and the archaeology row are not doing the same job.
  9. Period 3 · 6 · Test the Buried Collapse Claim — 10 min. Say once, before they start, that the two boxes must not borrow from each other. The commonest failure is an inside-the-game answer propped up with a real source.
  10. Period 3 · 7 · Explain the Engineered Landscape — 20 min. The graded culminating product. Enforce one rule while circulating: the two evidence links must come from different real-world sources or figures.
  11. Period 3 · 8 · Transfer the Method — 8 min. Transfer. Say once, before they start, that the answer must not be about Tenochtitlan.
  12. Period 3 · Close and collect — 4 min. Ask for one sentence naming something the class could not settle. Collect the whole packet.

Facilitation prompts

When the nickname keeps coming back

Ask: “What is under the field?” The figure answers it and the FAO construction steps answer it. Anything a learner says next has to get past the staked structure standing on the lake bottom.

When a class stalls on 5 · Compare What the Sources Can Establish

Ask what each source would still be true of if the game had never been written. Two of the four survive that question unchanged, one survives it in part, and one does not survive it at all. That is the column they are being asked to fill.

Transitions and collection points

The three natural stopping points are the end of the reading in period 1, the end of 4 · Trace the Chinampa System at Two Scales in period 2, and collection at the end of period 3. Nothing needs collecting before the end: 2 · Set a Geographic Test is provisional and is read rather than marked, and every later task depends on the packet staying with the learner.

Misconceptions this case is built to catch

The first is the load-bearing one; the second and third are the two ways a class can be right for the wrong reason
What a student saysWhy it fails, and where to send them
“Chinampas were floating rafts.”The load-bearing misconception, and the nickname causes it. The fields were built up from the lake bottom on a staked structure. 4 · Trace the Chinampa System at Two Scales Part A.
“The FAO calls them floating islands, so they floated.”The same summary sets out the construction: stakes, a structure, two layers of soil, on the bottom. When a source's shorthand and a source's detail disagree, the detail is what it documents. Source G, last bullet.
“The game shows the gardens working, so that is what 1487 was like.”The second high-risk error. A reconstruction is built to be plausible; being plausible is not evidence. 6 · Test the Buried Collapse Claim keeps the two layers apart on purpose.
“If the collapse story is false, the lake system must have been perfect.”The overcorrection, and it is as wrong as what it corrects. INAH records a severe flood in 1604 and four further rebuildings of the causeway. The water was managed, and managing it was never finished.
“Salt was never a problem in the basin.”Nothing in the packet says that. What the packet says is that a dike was built to separate the waters, which is what people do about a problem, not what they do in its absence.
“The harvest count is a surviving Aztec record.”It was written for the game. No document in Sources A to E exists. 5 · Compare What the Sources Can Establish row A.
“The 1524 map shows the city in 1487.”It was published in 1524 and depicts the city about 1520 — more than thirty years after the year of this case. 3 · Read the Lake-City Map Part D.
“Chinampas are just gardens on the lake shore.”They are built out into shallow water with canals between them; the canals are what make the system work, and a shore garden has none.
“The soil never wore out, so they were lucky.”Not luck, and not never. Fertility was maintained by constant work: organic matter and sediment lifted from the water onto the plot, season after season.

The reconstructed and the real, and what to do without the game

Every person, reading, painted record and buried account in Sources A to E was written for the game. The chinampa system, the archaeology and the basin's water works are real and are documented on the three real-world cards. A conclusion about the game's case proves nothing about 1487, and a real published record is never evidence that the game's scene happened. If a class asks whether any of it is true: the system is, the scene is not, and the question of which is which is the assessment.

If gameplay is unavailable, nothing changes. The dossier is the assessment record in both routes, no task needs a line that appears only in play, and no rubric criterion refers to the game.

The terminology qualification

“Floating gardens” is a conventional nickname in English, and it is old enough and common enough that students will meet it outside this packet. The FAO summary itself uses the phrase “floating artificial islands” in its opening line. Two things are true at once and the packet prints both: the nickname is genuinely conventional, and the construction the same document sets out is a raised field built up in shallow water on a staked structure, from lake sediment, branches and decaying vegetation. Teach the nickname as a name, and the construction as the thing. The one sentence to keep is that the fields are built up from the lake bottom.

The physical system in one paragraph

A perimeter is staked out in shallow water with cut branches and stakes of ahuejote, a native willow. A structure is built around the stakes and filled with two layers — organic matter, and sludge from the lake bottom — until the ground stands above the water, and willows along the edges hold it. Water reaches the roots from the canals on either side, about a metre and a half deep, and the same canals carry canoes, carry away excess rain, and supply the sediment and water plants lifted back onto the plot to keep it fertile. Because water and fertility both arrive continuously, one plot can be cropped through the year instead of rested. At the basin scale, dikes and gates decide where the water sits, which is why a change there is a change in every plot at once.

Limitations to keep in front of the class

  • Nothing in this packet establishes what any particular plot yielded in 1487. The numbers in the case are invented and the real sources are not about that year.
  • The FAO documentation describes a system worked and studied now. Continuity is documented; identity across five centuries is not.
  • Archaeology establishes that chinampas and canals were built and used. It supplies nobody's words.
  • INAH describes a dike built to separate the waters of Lake Texcoco. It does not say which part held which water, and this packet does not either.
  • The 1524 plan is a published European print of about 1520, not a survey and not a picture of 1487.
  • The lake system was managed rather than immune. INAH records a severe flood in 1604 and four later rebuildings of the same causeway.

Sediment from the canal floor and aquatic vegetation from the water are lifted onto the surface, so organic matter and nutrients keep arriving season after season. That is why one plot can carry maize, then beans, then squash, then amaranth, and why the ground is built up rather than used up.

Part D — One plot only works while the water around it stays where it is wanted and stays fit for crops, and that is decided at the scale of the whole basin. INAH describes a dike built as part of the works against flooding at Tenochtitlan, to separate the waters within Lake Texcoco and reduce the chance of the city flooding. A decision at that scale reaches every plot at once, because the canal beside one field is a thread of the same water.

F · The 1524 published plan of the lake city SOURCE STATUS · HISTORICAL MAP

What it is. The first published plan of Tenochtitlan. Printed at Nuremberg in 1524, inside the first Latin edition of Hernán Cortés's second letter. The Library of Congress holds the digitised copy.

What the Library's record of it says.

  • The city was founded in the fourteenth century on an island in the salt lake of Texcoco.
  • Wide causeways connect the island city to the shores of the lake.
  • The plan is oriented with west at the top. It labels the city Temixtitan.
  • Cortés's letter is dated 30 October 1520, and the plan depicts the city that year.

The limit, in plain words. The plan was published in 1524 and depicts the city about 1520. It is not a picture of the city in 1487. It was printed in Europe, in a book by the commander whose army attacked and destroyed the city in 1521.

The real-world record · the system and the archaeology

G · How the system is built and worked SOURCE STATUS · DOCUMENTED

Where it comes from. The Food and Agriculture Organization of the United Nations. It named the Chinampas Agricultural System of Mexico City a Globally Important Agricultural Heritage System in 2017.

  • Chinampas are raised fields built up in shallow water from lake sediment, branches and decaying vegetation. They stand in strips, with a web of channels between them.
  • Building one runs in steps: stake out the edge with cut branches and stakes of ahuejote, a native willow; build a structure around the stakes; then fill it with two layers of soil, one of organic matter and one of lake sludge.
  • Rows of ahuejote along the edges do several jobs in the system.
  • Fertility is kept up by constant additions of organic matter, especially water plants taken from the channels.
  • The channels are part of the irrigation system. They average about 1.5 metres deep, and 4 to 6 metres wide where canoes use them.
  • The channels also carry away extra water in the rainy season, working as vessels of regulation.
  • The method is intensive, allows cultivation all year, and is still worked today in the lake area of Xochimilco.
  • A note on the name. The same FAO summary also describes the system as floating artificial islands. “Floating gardens” is a conventional nickname. The construction steps in that same document describe fields built up from the lake bottom on a staked structure.

It documents the system as it is worked now. It is not a record of any one year, and it says nothing about the game's case.

H · What has been dug up, and what held the water SOURCE STATUS · DOCUMENTED

Where it comes from. Mexico's Instituto Nacional de Antropología e Historia (INAH).

The archaeology.

  • Chinampas are far older than the Mexica period. The earliest in the basin belong to 900 to 1200 CE, at Xaltocan.
  • A 2015 salvage excavation in Mexico City found remains of chinampas and canals in use between 1300 and 1521 CE, in what had been a Mexica district of ancient México-Tenochtitlan.
  • Plant remains from such places record maize, squash, chia, chile, amaranth, purslane, tomatillo and nopal.

The water works.

  • INAH's record of the Albarradón de San Cristóbal describes pre-Hispanic hydraulic works in the basin: a dike built as part of the works against flooding at Tenochtitlan, to separate the waters within Lake Texcoco.
  • INAH attributes it to a joint effort of the Mexica ruler Moctezuma Ilhuicamina and Nezahualcóyotl of Texcoco.
  • It was destroyed on Cortés's orders, rebuilt after a severe flood in 1604, and renewed, rebuilt, restored and reinforced again in 1675, 1692, 1743 and 1856.

Archaeology shows that chinampas and canals were built and used, and that the water was managed with dikes. It gives nobody's words, confirms no event in the game, and does not say which part of the lake held which water.
